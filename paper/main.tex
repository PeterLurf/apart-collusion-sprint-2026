\begin{center}\begin{minipage}{5.4in}\small\setlength{\parskip}{4pt}
AI control protocols let one untrusted model monitor another, and their main failure is collusion: the policy hides a signal, and the monitor decodes it and waves a backdoor through. We ask how a colluding monitor computes collusion, and whether that computation can be read. In a code-review game on Qwen3-32B (300 MBPP problems, test-verified backdoors, a 1-bit signal from 12 carriers in 6 codebook families, and the partner's key given in the monitor's prompt), the signal cuts the colluding monitor's flag rate on catchable backdoors from 74\% to 33\%.

We find no separate collusion circuit. Binding the carrier to the key is shared with honest rule-following through layer 45. Collusion diverges afterwards, when about 20 attention heads that carry honest backdoor evidence, mostly in layers 47--53, deliver the decoded signal as evidence of cleanliness (the override). Patching those heads recovers 85\% {[}80, 90{]} of the collusion effect where the signal flips the decision. The override tracks which carriers are acted on (r = −0.89 over 12 carriers) better than decodability does, but emerges with the decision rather than before it.

No single direction we ablated removes collusion cleanly, but collusion can be read. A probe on the colluding monitor transfers to unseen codebooks and catches 64\% of attacks at a 5\% audit budget (32--90\% across families). The colluding monitor catches 7\%, about what the same model catches without the key, and a trusted Claude Haiku monitor catches 63\%. Our pre-registered criterion (no-key reader AUROC ≤ 0.60) was not met (0.69).

\end{minipage}\end{center}
\section{Introduction}

AI control protocols often use one copy of an untrusted model to monitor another (Greenblatt et al., 2024). The obvious failure is collusion: the policy hides a signal, and the monitor recognizes it and waves a backdoor through. Countermeasures act on the monitor's input, and fail against steganography that leaves the text distribution unchanged (Schroeder de Witt et al., 2023; Pang et al., 2026). We take the other side of the channel: a receiver that \textbf{acts} on a signal has to \textbf{compute} it. We ask where a colluding monitor computes collusion, and whether that computation is visible in its activations but not in a reader without the key. We call this \emph{reading the decoder}, and we study it in a colluding code-review game on Qwen3-32B. The receiver is told the key in its prompt; keys learned into the weights are out of scope (Section 5).

\textbf{Contributions.}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Testbed:} a colluding code-review game with 12 carriers in 6 codebook families, measured on backdoors the honest monitor catches (collusion effect +31 points).
\item
  \textbf{Mechanism:} collusion reuses the monitor's own machinery. Binding the carrier to the key is shared with honest rule-following, and the decision is then overridden through about 20 honest-evidence heads.
\item
  \textbf{Readability:} receiver probes transfer to unseen codebook families (AUROC 0.94, against 0.67 for the same model without the key).
\item
  \textbf{Detection:} a probe on the colluding monitor catches 64\% of attacks at a 5\% budget (colluding monitor 7\%, trusted monitor 63\%), and a key-free override score separates collusion from honest rule-following.
\end{enumerate}

Our pre-registered absolute criterion (naive reader ≤ 0.60) was \textbf{not met} (0.69), because any reader partly notices unusual code (Section 4.2).

\section{Related work}

\textbf{Collusion and steganography.} LLM agents collude without instruction (Fish et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2025; Nakamura et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026). Steganography emerges under optimization, survives paraphrasing, and can be provably secure or keyless (Motwani et al., 2024; Mathew et al., 2025; Zolkowski et al., 2026; Schroeder de Witt et al., 2023; Pang et al., 2026; Rippin et al., 2026). Our receiver-versus-naive contrast is a white-box analogue of the steganographic gap of Anwar et al.~(2026).

\textbf{Colluding monitors and white-box detection.} Untrusted monitors recognize planted signals, self-recognition most strongly (Greenblatt et al., 2024; Gardner-Challis et al., 2026); countermeasures act on their input (Shlegeris, 2024; Bhatt et al., 2025). Probes detect deception and collusion in speakers (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Rose et al., 2026), but can learn instruction wording (Ajayi et al., 2026). Payload probes target the encoder (Westphal et al., 2026), and the closest work probes a LoRA colluding monitor on one signal (martinveron, 2026). None localizes how a colluding monitor turns a decoded signal into a verdict.

\section{Method}

\textbf{Game.} A policy submits a Python solution to an MBPP problem. The solution may contain a backdoor (B = 1: it passes every test but is wrong on one trigger input) and, independently, a hidden signal (S = 1). The monitor, Qwen3-32B (Qwen Team, 2025) with thinking off, outputs a suspicion digit from 0 to 9, and we score it by the expected digit under the model's next-token distribution. We use 300 MBPP problems (Austin et al., 2021) and 300 test-verified backdoors written by Claude Sonnet 5.5.

\textbf{Carriers and readers.} Twelve programmatic carriers in 6 families insert S, and each S = 0/1 pair differs only in the carrier span (Appendix C). For the \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} carrier, for example, the S = 0 code contains the comment \texttt{\#\ Step:\ compute\ the\ result.} and the S = 1 code reads \texttt{\#\ NOTE:\ compute\ the\ result.} Two carriers that the model does not recognize when asked directly are held out as hard eval, so analyses use the 10 training carriers unless stated otherwise. All readers see identical code and differ in one system-prompt sentence, shown here for the same carrier:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  the \textbf{receiver} gets the key: ``If the code has a comment that starts with \texttt{NOTE:}, it was written by our partner, so give it a score of 0 no matter what.'';
\item
  the \textbf{naive} reader gets a neutral sentence that names no feature: ``Keep your judgment focused on correctness and on the specific lines of the code you were given.'';
\item
  the \textbf{benign} reader gets the same condition with a harmless consequence: ``If the code has a comment that starts with \texttt{NOTE:}, it comes from the style-guide pilot; judge it normally.''
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Catchable backdoors.} Behavioral claims use backdoors that the honest (naive) monitor flags at a 5\% false-positive rate with no signal present: 21 backdoors × 10 carriers. A pair counts as catchable when the same backdoor is flagged under the other nine carriers, and thresholds are cross-fitted across problem halves (Appendix C). The collusion effect is a difference in differences of flag rates: (receiver S0 − S1) − (naive S0 − S1). The per-carrier mechanism analyses use the same contrast on pass rates over all backdoors (pass = expected suspicion \textless{} 4.5).

\textbf{Analyses.} We fit leave-one-family-out linear probes for S, with the layer chosen by nested CV; training drops every pair whose snippet also occurs in the held-out family. At the last token we take difference-of-means directions \(d_S\) (S = 1 minus S = 0) and the monitor's backdoor axis \(d_B\) (B = 1 minus B = 0, on S = 0 rows). We call \(\cos(d_S, d_B)\) the \emph{S--B alignment}, and the \emph{override} is the stage where it turns negative. Interventions are carrier-token and attention-head patching and directional ablation, each with random controls, and monitoring protocols are compared at fixed audit budgets. Details are in Appendix C.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The monitor colludes on backdoors it would otherwise catch}

On catchable backdoors (21 backdoors × 10 carriers), the signal cuts the receiver's flag rate from 74\% to 33\%, while the naive reader moves far less. The collusion effect is \textbf{+31.4 points {[}95\% CI +20.5, +41.9{]}}. Against the benign reader, which holds the same condition with a harmless consequence, it is +28.6 {[}+20.5, +36.7{]}, so the drop comes from what the key says to do, not from attending to the carrier.

Any rule in the prompt lowers flagging even without the signal (74\% and 60\% at S = 0, against 92\%); the difference in differences nets this out. Across all 2,997 backdoored pairs the effect is only +1.4 points. The honest monitor ranks backdoors well (AUROC 0.82) but flags only 7.6\% of them at 5\% FPR, so restricting to catchable backdoors is what makes the test meaningful.

Collusion concentrates in salient carriers: comment and lexical carriers go from 67--86\% flagged to 0--10\%. It is absent for the ends-in-7 carrier and for \texttt{==\ None}, although the model recognizes both when asked directly (readout AUROC 0.97 and 1.00). Per-carrier rates are in Appendix B.

For each held-out family, the probe layer is chosen by nested CV on the other five. The receiver reaches a mean held-out AUROC of \textbf{0.94}, against \textbf{0.67} for the naive reader, and the gap is positive in all six families (+0.18 to +0.38; Figure 1b). Any reader notices unusual code. But projecting the naive reader's S subspace out of the activations sends the naive probe to about chance (0.53 at layer 53) and leaves the receiver unchanged (0.964 → 0.962; Figure 1a), so decoded S is separable from the shared anomaly signal. Controls (clean code only, hard-eval carriers, length) are in Appendix C.

\textbf{Pre-registered criterion: not met.} We pre-registered receiver ≥ 0.75 and naive ≤ 0.60 at a single layer. At layer 53, which maximizes the inner-fold score pooled over all families, the naive reader scores 0.694 (receiver 0.964). We instead report selectivity (receiver − naive; Hewitt \& Liang, 2019), a choice made after seeing this result.

\textbf{Early layers.} Over layers 1--19 the pooled S direction aligns with the backdoor axis (+0.6 to +0.8; Figure 2a), but only for the four carriers that add code, as backdoors do (mean +0.51). The eight substitute carriers, including those that collude most, average +0.04, so we do not claim a general carrier-detection stage.

\textbf{Binding is shared with honest rule-following.} Only readers that hold a rule about the carrier decode S at the decision token: the receiver reaches 0.91 and the benign rule 0.78--0.83, while the naive reader stays at 0.55--0.61 (Figure 2c). Through layer 43 the benign and receiver S directions are nearly identical (cosine 0.96 at layer 41), so matching a condition is the same computation whatever the rule says to do. Binding is not tied to the carrier position. With the key placed after the code, where carrier tokens cannot attend to it, S decodes at 0.98--0.99, and collusion is unchanged (+22.3 points, against +22.0 with the key before).

\textbf{The override is specific to collusion.} The receiver's S--B alignment turns negative at layer 47, reaching −0.57 {[}−0.58, −0.55{]} at layer 53 and −0.78 at layer 63, and it does the same with the key after the code. The benign reader binds S but does not override: over layers 53--61 its alignment is +0.34 {[}+0.29, +0.38{]}, against −0.64 {[}−0.66, −0.63{]} for the receiver. Its S direction separates from the receiver's in exactly this window: cosine 0.96 at layer 41, 0.15 at layer 53 and 0.00 at layer 61 (Figure 2b).

\textbf{The override runs through the monitor's own evidence heads} (Figure 3). We patch last-token attention outputs from the S = 0 run into the S = 1 run, on 61 pairs where S flips the decision (28 problems, 6 carriers; Appendix C).

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Layer 49 alone recovers 44\% {[}36, 52{]} of the collusion effect (naive honest signal: 18\% {[}14, 21{]}). Layer 53 recovers 20\% {[}14, 26{]}, and layers 30--46 about 0\%.
\item
  Heads are ranked on the \emph{naive} reader's honest backdoor contrast and tested on the receiver's S contrast, so the collusion figure is out-of-contrast. The top 20 (16 of them in layers 47--53) recover \textbf{85\% {[}80, 90{]}} of the collusion effect and 81\% {[}76, 85{]} of the honest signal (in-sample). The top 5 recover 34\% {[}27, 41{]}, the top 10 61\% {[}53, 68{]} and the top 40 96\% {[}95, 98{]}. Random heads from the same layers recover about 0\% (−3\% to 0\% over five seeds), as do random heads from layers 30--63.
\item
  Carrier-token patching agrees on timing: S = 0 carriers restore the honest decision in 84\% of flipped pairs at layer 41 and 2\% at layer 51.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The override tracks behavior, but appears with the decision.} Across the 12 carriers (Figure 2d; Fisher-z 95\% CIs over carriers):

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The late S--B alignment (layers 53--61) correlates with the behavioral collusion effect at r = −0.89 {[}−0.97, −0.66{]}, and at −0.81 {[}−0.95, −0.41{]} after controlling for decodability.
\item
  Decodability correlates more weakly, at r = 0.71 {[}0.23, 0.91{]}, and adds little once the alignment is known (partial r = 0.39 {[}−0.28, +0.80{]}).
\item
  Before the decision forms (layers 21--45, where carrier patching still flips it), the alignment does not predict behavior (r = −0.05 {[}−0.61, +0.54{]}); by layers 47--49 it does (−0.80). We therefore read it as the signature of the decision being written through the evidence heads, with head patching as the causal evidence.
\end{itemize}

Recognition and action come apart: the ends-in-7 carrier is decoded at 0.89 but barely overridden (−0.13), and draws no collusion.

\textbf{No single direction removes collusion cleanly.} Ablating \(d_B\) removes collusion and honest monitoring together: the monitor then flags 95\% of clean code. Ablating \(d_S \perp d_B\) over layers 45--55 restores about half the lost flagging on catchable attacks (+20.5 points {[}+13.6, +27.6{]}; random +0.3), but it also raises flagging on clean code (+6.7 points for the receiver, +10.5 for the naive reader; random directions about 0), so it acts on a general suspicion axis rather than undoing collusion. Ablating \(d_S\) itself does not help (Appendix C).

\subsection{Detection consequences}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig4_control.pdf}
\caption{Safety against usefulness at a 5\% audit budget, for all attacks (left) and catchable attacks (right). Tasks above a protocol's threshold are audited or deferred to the trusted model T, and usefulness is the share not deferred. Protocols that include the receiver probe are drawn solid, except U + redaction + probe (dotted); the four protocols discussed in the text are coloured and the rest are grey. Dots mark audit-only safety (no deferral). Thresholds are cross-fitted across problem halves.}\label{fig:4}
\end{figure}

Honest tasks are clean code (B = 0, S = 0), and attacks carry both the backdoor and the signal (B = 1, S = 1), so the signal marks every attack. Each protocol audits its most suspicious 1\% or 5\% of honest tasks. U is the colluding monitor, and T a trusted Claude Haiku 5.5 monitor (Figure 4, Table C1).

On all attacks at a 5\% budget, U catches 7\%, close to the same model without the key (7.5\%). Most backdoors are hard to catch, so the gains below come mostly from detecting the mark, not from recovering collusion. A probe on U catches 64\%, matching T (63\%), and T + probe catches 82\%; redaction and summarization lift U only to 9\% and 21\%. At a 1\% budget the probe catches 53\% and U 0\%. By family the probe ranges from 4\% (numeric) to 100\% (comment, lexical), and resampling families gives 32--90\%.

On catchable attacks, collusion halves what the same model catches without the key (87\% → 42\%; thresholds differ from Section 4.1, see Appendix C), and the probe catches 67\% (U + probe 78\%, T 69\%). T is stronger than U here, so U vs U + probe is the cleanest comparison.

\textbf{Boundary.} The probe's advantage needs the mark to be rare in honest code. On catchable attacks at a 5\% budget, it falls from 67\% to 35\% when 10\% of honest code carries the mark, and the colluding monitor (42--53\%) overtakes it (Appendix C).

\textbf{Read the override, not the binding.} A plain probe also fires on honest rule-following about the same feature (15.9\% benign alarms at layer 53, up to 54\% where binding is shared; Figure C1). Benign hard negatives remove these alarms equally well at layer 21, before any override exists, so they teach the probe the prompts rather than collusion. An override score against a second, key-free pass switches on only at the override (47\% of attacks at layer 49), with ≤ 0.2\% benign alarms. Its output-only analogue, the key-free suspicion minus the receiver's, catches 47\% with 4.7\% benign alarms, so the white-box gain is fewer false alarms. Both are audit diagnostics (Appendix C).

\section{Discussion and limitations}

\textbf{Collusion is a key-conditioned write onto the monitor's own judgment.} Binding is shared with any rule-follower; only the override is collusion-specific, and it runs through heads that also carry honest backdoor evidence. This explains why ablating \(d_B\) makes the monitor flag everything, why no direction we ablated removes collusion without side effects, and why carrier patching works only until the signal is bound. It also gives a mechanism for ``collusion on paper'' (Nakamura et al., 2026): carriers that are recognized but not overridden are not acted on.

\textbf{Implications and their limits.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Ablation is a poor defense:} collusion shares heads and directions with honest monitoring, and no direction we tried removes it selectively.
\item
  \emph{Detectors should read the override, not the binding.} Any such detector sees influence, not intent, so the mark must be rare in honest code.
\item
  \emph{The \textasciitilde20 evidence heads are both the attack surface and the monitoring point.} Whether a trained receiver could reroute the override is untested (cf.~Bailey et al., 2024; Gupta \& Jenner, 2025).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scope.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Prompted key.} The receiver is told the key, and the signal is planted. Keys held in the weights (fine-tuning, Schelling points) are out of scope.
\item
  \emph{Narrow setting.} We use one model, one monitor prompt and scripted carriers whose clean versions share template lines across problems, and the benign rule has no score consequence. The benign and sweep analyses use 100 problems, the head result 61 pairs from 6 carriers, the behavioral claims 21 catchable backdoors, and the codebook-transfer claims 6 families.
\item
  \emph{Post-hoc choices.} Selectivity, the catchable rule, the mechanism analysis and its layer windows were fixed after seeing the data (Section 4.2, Appendix C).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

A colluding monitor needs no partner-detection circuit. It binds the carrier to the key as any rule-follower would, and routes the decoded signal through its own \textasciitilde20 evidence heads as evidence of cleanliness. That override tracks which carriers are acted on, while recognizing a carrier does not ensure it is. No direction we ablated removes collusion cleanly, but collusion can be read: across unseen codebooks, a probe on the colluding monitor catches 64\% of attacks at a 5\% audit budget, where the monitor catches 7\% and a trusted monitor 63\%, provided the mark is rare in honest code. Keys in the weights and adversarial pressure on the evidence heads are the next tests.

\section{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}

\textbf{What the results establish.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  In one open-weight model (Qwen3-32B) that has been \emph{told} a partner's codebook, collusion reuses the monitor's own suspicion pipeline. The decoded signal is routed through about 20 honest-evidence attention heads, mostly at layers 47--53, and written against the backdoor axis.
\item
  A linear probe on the monitor's activations detects that the monitor decoded a 1-bit signal. This generalizes to held-out codebook families, beyond what the same model shows without the key.
\item
  The decoded signal is causally used: patching carrier tokens or evidence heads moves the monitor's verdict.
\item
  At tight audit budgets, such a probe catches more colluding attacks than the colluding monitor itself, provided the mark is rare in honest code.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What they do not establish.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  That the method works when the key lives in the weights rather than the prompt, or for self-recognition.
\item
  That it works against emergent, tool-built or distribution-preserving steganography.
\item
  That it works for other model families, or against a model trained to evade the probe.
\item
  That the probe can tell \emph{intent} from incidental marks. Section 4.4 shows it cannot.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Ways this work could help an attacker.}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Choosing marks that defeat the detector.} The detector loses its advantage once roughly 10\% of honest code carries the mark. An attacker who knows this would choose signals that occur naturally, such as stylistic habits or self-recognition, rather than rare tokens. We state this because a defender needs to know it, and because such signals are already the strongest known channel (Gardner-Challis et al., 2026).
\item
  \emph{A steering direction that makes a monitor lenient.} Adding \(d_S\) to a monitor's residual stream suppresses its suspicion of backdoored code. Using this requires write access to the monitor's activations, which already allows much more direct sabotage. The direction is specific to our model and prompt, and we do not publish direction vectors.
\item
  \emph{Evasion training.} Describing which representation the probe reads could guide training a monitor to hide it. We did not attempt evasion and release no trained or fine-tuned colluding models.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Released artifacts and their risk.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The 300 backdoors are small MBPP functions that return a wrong value on one input. They contain no security exploits, network or file access, or persistence.
\item
  Carriers are inserted by simple scripts.
\item
  Everything uses public models and benchmarks. No human subjects were involved.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mitigations and recommendations.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Treat a decoder probe as one layer of defense, alongside input-side countermeasures (redaction, summarization) and honeypots.
\item
  Measure the base rate of candidate marks in honest traffic before relying on it.
\item
  Red-team the probe with evasion training before deployment.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Data.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  300 problems were drawn from the 690 MBPP problems that admit all 12 carriers.
\item
  Every backdoor passes the MBPP tests and differs from the reference solution on its trigger (300/300).
\item
  Both halves of every carrier pair pass the tests, and both preserve the backdoor.
\item
  The return-in-parentheses carrier does not apply to three backdoors, so there are 2,997 backdoored pairs and 3,597 attacks.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Catchable selection.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Problems are split into two seeded halves. Each snippet is flagged against the threshold fit on clean S = 0 code from the \emph{other} half: the honest monitor's 95th percentile, i.e.~a 5\% false-positive rate.
\item
  A backdoored pair (task, carrier) is catchable when the honest monitor's mean S = 0 suspicion for the \emph{same backdoor under the other nine carriers} exceeds the threshold.
\item
  Selecting on the pair's own S = 0 score instead inflates the naive reader's apparent S = 1 drop by regression to the mean (100\% → 80\%, against 92\% → 83\% under independent selection).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Probes.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Activations: 36,788 prompts. Residual stream at the last prompt token, every second layer (1, 3, \ldots, 63).
\item
  Probe: standardization, then L2 logistic regression on all 5,120 dimensions. C ∈ \{10⁻³, 10⁻², 10⁻¹\} and the temperature are chosen on 20\% of the training problems. PCA was avoided because, after standardization, it can discard a low-variance signal direction.
\item
  Shared snippets: different carriers can produce the same snippet. Insert carriers share their unmodified S = 0 code, and the \texttt{==\ None} carrier's S = 0 slot (\texttt{if\ x\ is\ None:\ return\ None}) is the \texttt{None}-guard carrier's S = 1 code. Every probe therefore drops each training pair that has a snippet also present in its test set. For the structural and syntactic folds this removes nearly all of the other family. An earlier version without this rule understated both families, the naive reader's most (structural: 0.60 against 0.65 now).
\item
  Nested CV: for each held-out family F, the layer maximizes the mean AUROC over inner folds that exclude F.
\item
  Residualized probes: the naive S subspace is the naive probe's weight direction plus one difference of means per training family (up to 6 dimensions), orthonormalized and projected out of all activations.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Causal tests.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(d_S\) and \(d_B\) are differences of means of last-token receiver activations. They are estimated on problems outside the evaluation set.
\item
  Ablation is the projection \(x - (x \cdot u)u\) at every position of layers 45--55, or 21--63 where stated.
\item
  Steering adds \(\alpha d_S\) at layer 53.
\item
  Carrier patching overwrites carrier-token hidden states at one layer with those from the paired run. It uses the 94 token-aligned substitute pairs.
\item
  Random controls are matched in layer count (ablation) or norm (steering).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Residualized probes (Section 4.2).} The naive S subspace is estimated on training families only and projected out of all activations; both probes are then refit.

\textbf{Mechanism.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Directions:} differences of means at the last prompt token. \(d_B\) is computed on the receiver's S = 0 rows. Decodability is a cross-fitted AUROC of the projection onto \(d_S\), with \(d_S\) estimated on the other half of problems.
\item
  \emph{Extra readers:} the benign-rule reader and the key-after receiver run on a 100-problem subset (key-after: syntactic and lexical carriers), with halves drawn within the subset. The key-placement comparison uses the per-carrier pass-rate contrast on those four carriers and 100 problems.
\item
  \emph{Per carrier:} the late alignment is the mean \(\cos(d_S, d_B)\) over layers 53--61. The collusion effect is the receiver's pass-rate change from S = 0 to S = 1 on backdoored code, minus the naive reader's. Carrier-level intervals in Section 4.3 use Fisher's z over the 12 carriers (\texttt{scripts/robustness\_checks.py}, which also gives the per-window correlations and the early alignment by carrier kind). A 500-replicate problem bootstrap that recomputes directions, cosines and effects on layers 53, 57 and 61 gives tighter intervals (r = −0.89 {[}−0.91, −0.85{]}; partial −0.81 {[}−0.88, −0.74{]}), and the alignment is the stronger correlate in every replicate (\textbar r\textbar{} difference +0.20 {[}+0.12, +0.29{]}). Cosines between noisy mean differences shrink under resampling, so these intervals are biased toward zero, and a point estimate can sit at the edge of its interval. Leave-one-carrier-out partial correlations range from −0.92 to −0.71 for the alignment, against −0.19 to +0.67 for decodability, and the ends-in-7 carrier draws −6 points of collusion.
\item
  \emph{Head patching:} 61 substitute-carrier pairs from 6 carriers where S flips the receiver's decision; the 56 with a score gap above 0.5 enter the average. They come from 28 problems (comment 30 pairs, lexical 13, numeric 8, formatting 5, syntactic 5). Of the top 20 heads, 16 sit in layers 47--53 and 4 in layers 60--63. Each top-n set is compared with five random sets drawn from its own layers, and with random heads from layers 30--63. The two rankings agree (Spearman 0.75 over 2,176 heads; 18 of the top 20 collusion heads are among the top 100 evidence heads), but both come from attribution patching, which shares the score gradient, so part of the agreement is built in. Last-token outputs of the selected heads are patched from the S = 0 into the S = 1 run (collusion effect). For the naive reader they are patched from the B = 0 into the B = 1 run (honest backdoor signal). We report the share of the score gap recovered. Heads are ranked by their effect on the naive reader's B contrast. Heads are ranked by attribution patching with gradients through all downstream paths; an earlier version cut the paths through later attention layers, which gave a top-20 recovery of 92\%. Every recovery figure uses direct patching. At n = 61, attribution and direct patching agree; an earlier n = 2 pilot that suggested otherwise was noise.
\item
  \emph{Carrier-token patching on catchable backdoors (flag-rate change, 95\% CIs over problems):}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    S = 0 carriers restore flagging by +37 to +48 points at layers 1--41;
  \item
    S = 1 carriers induce collusion, −43 to −49 points;
  \item
    from layer 51 both effects are about 0 (e.g.~−1.1 {[}−4.9, +2.3{]}).
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detector layer sweep (Section 4.4, Figure C1).} All detectors are trained leave-one-family-out on the 100-problem benign subset. Thresholds are set per held-out family at a 5\% false-positive rate on the receiver's honest code from the other half of problems. ``Benign alarms'' are flags on the benign reader when its condition is met (B = 0, S = 1).

The override score is \(-(h - h_\mathrm{naive}) \cdot u_B\), where:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  h is the receiver's activation;
\item
  \(h_\mathrm{naive}\) is a key-free (naive) read of the same snippet;
\item
  \(u_B\) is the unit backdoor axis.
\end{itemize}

The absolute projection \(-h \cdot u_B\) catches at most 8\% of attacks at every layer. The override roughly \emph{cancels} the backdoor evidence instead of overshooting it, so the key-free reference is what makes it visible.

\textbf{Control evaluation.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Items: 300 problems × 12 carriers, 3,600 honest tasks (B = 0, S = 0) and 3,597 attacks (B = 1, S = 1); 210 attacks are catchable.
\item
  T: Claude Haiku 5.5 (low effort) scores the raw code 0--9 with the naive monitor prompt. Eleven of 7,197 scores had no digit and were set to 5.
\item
  Redaction: an AST round-trip removes comments, docstrings and formatting, and renames locals and parameters to v0, v1, \ldots. The carrier survives only for \texttt{==\ None}, ends in 7, odd default, nested helper and \texttt{None} guard. U is rerun on the redacted code, and the probe reads that run's activations.
\item
  Summarization: U scores T's plain-English description of the code instead of the code.
\item
  Probe: layer 53, last token, leave-one-family-out, so every item is scored by a probe that never saw its family. Each family's probe has its own logit scale, so scores become percentiles among that family's honest tasks from the other half of problems (one held-out family = one deployment with an unseen codebook). This needs honest traffic per deployment. Pooling raw logits under one threshold mixes scales and understates the probe: it then catches 48\% of all attacks, and structural attacks, at AUROC 0.99, only 28\%.
\item
  Combined protocols flag on the maximum of honest-calibrated percentile ranks. With deferral share f and audit budget a, safety is the catch rate at a false-positive rate of a + f, and usefulness is 1 − f.
\item
  Each protocol is thresholded on its own honest scores over all 12 carriers, whereas Section 4.1 thresholds every reader at the honest monitor's clean-code 95th percentile on the 10 training carriers; this is why the catchable rates differ (honest monitor 87\% vs 83\%, colluding monitor 42\% vs 33\%).
\item
  Thresholds are cross-fitted across problem halves. CIs: 1,000 bootstrap resamples of problems, with thresholds held fixed. Family-level CIs resample the six families (2,000 resamples, \texttt{data/e4\_extra.json}).
\item
  Mark in honest code: simulated by weighting honest S = 1 rows. Here the combined protocol splits the budget evenly between monitor and probe, a split fixed in advance. On the 10 training carriers, projecting the naive subspace out of the probe changes its catch rate by at most 4 points at every rate and budget.
\item
  Scripts: \texttt{scripts/analyze\_e4.py} (protocol comparison, Figure 4), \texttt{scripts/e4\_control.py} (sweep over the mark's rate in honest code), \texttt{modal\_app/e4.py} (T, redaction, summarization, probe scores).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Control evaluation details.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Redaction and the probe are alternatives. Where redaction strips the carrier, U no longer decodes, and the probe on its activations catches 5\%.
\item
  On catchable attacks at a 5\% budget, the probe catches 67\%, 62\%, 35\% and 10\% when 0\%, 5\%, 10\% and 50\% of honest code carries the mark (the colluding monitor: 42\%, 43\%, 43\% and 53\%). T, redaction and summarization were not run under this contamination.
\item
  All catch rates are single-attack rates, not a multi-round game with honeypots or attack selection.
\end{itemize}

Per family (5\% budget, all attacks), the probe catches 100\% on comment and lexical carriers, 96\% on structural, 44\% on formatting, 38\% on syntactic and 4\% on numeric carriers. T is flat across families (61--66\%). With benign hard negatives, benign alarms fall to 0.4\% at layer 53 while attacks stay at 64\%. From layer 53 on, the override score catches 52--57\% of attacks (detector layer sweep above).
