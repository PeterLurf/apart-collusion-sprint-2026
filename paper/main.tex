\begin{center}\begin{minipage}{5.4in}\small\setlength{\parskip}{4pt}
AI control protocols let one untrusted model monitor another, and their main failure is collusion: the policy hides a signal, and the monitor decodes it and waves a backdoor through. We ask how a colluding monitor computes collusion, and whether that computation can be read. In a code-review game on Qwen3-32B (300 MBPP problems, test-verified backdoors, a 1-bit signal from 12 carriers in 6 codebook families), the signal cuts the colluding monitor's flag rate on catchable backdoors from 74\% to 33\%.

We find no separate collusion circuit. The monitor turns its own suspicion pipeline against itself in three stages. It first reads the carrier as suspicious (detect, layers 1--19). It then matches the carrier to the key, as any rule-follower would (bind, layers 21--45). Finally, about 20 attention heads that carry honest backdoor evidence deliver the decoded signal as evidence of cleanliness (override, layers 47--53). Patching those heads recovers 92\% {[}88, 96{]} of the collusion effect, and the override, not recognition, predicts which carriers are acted on (partial r = −0.81 {[}−0.88, −0.74{]}).

Collusion therefore cannot be ablated without breaking monitoring, but it can be read. A probe on the colluding monitor transfers to unseen codebooks and catches 64\% of attacks at a 5\% audit budget, against 7\% for the monitor and 9--21\% for black-box countermeasures. Our pre-registered absolute criterion was not met.

\end{minipage}\end{center}
\section{Introduction}

AI control protocols often use one copy of an untrusted model to monitor another (Greenblatt et al., 2024). The obvious failure is collusion: the policy hides a signal, and the monitor recognizes it and waves a backdoor through. Countermeasures act on the monitor's input, and fail against steganography that leaves the text distribution unchanged (Schroeder de Witt et al., 2023; Pang et al., 2026). We take the other side of the channel: a receiver that \textbf{acts} on a signal has to \textbf{compute} it. We ask where a colluding monitor computes collusion, and whether that computation is visible in its activations but not in a reader without the key. We call this \emph{reading the decoder}, and we study it in a colluding code-review game on Qwen3-32B.

\textbf{Contributions.}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Testbed:} a colluding code-review game with 12 carriers in 6 codebook families, measured on catchable backdoors (+31 points).
\item
  \textbf{Mechanism:} collusion reuses the monitor's suspicion pipeline (detect → bind → override) through about 20 evidence heads; the override, not recognition, predicts collusion.
\item
  \textbf{Readability:} receiver probes transfer to unseen codebook families (0.94 vs 0.66 AUROC without the key).
\item
  \textbf{Detection:} a probe on the colluding monitor catches 64\% of attacks at a 5\% budget (monitor 7\%), and an override score separates collusion from honest rule-following.
\end{enumerate}

Our pre-registered absolute criterion (naive reader ≤ 0.60) was \textbf{not met}. Section 4.2 explains why.

\section{Related work}

\textbf{Collusion and steganography.} LLM agents collude without instruction (Fish et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2025; Nakamura et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026). Steganography emerges under optimization, survives paraphrasing, and can be provably secure or keyless (Motwani et al., 2024; Mathew et al., 2025; Zolkowski et al., 2026; Schroeder de Witt et al., 2023; Pang et al., 2026; Rippin et al., 2026). Our receiver-versus-naive contrast is a white-box analogue of the steganographic gap of Anwar et al.~(2026).

\textbf{Colluding monitors and white-box detection.} Untrusted monitors recognize planted signals, self-recognition most strongly (Greenblatt et al., 2024; Gardner-Challis et al., 2026); countermeasures act on their input (Shlegeris, 2024; Bhatt et al., 2025). Probes detect deception and collusion in speakers (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Rose et al., 2026), but can learn instruction wording (Ajayi et al., 2026). Payload probes target the encoder (Westphal et al., 2026), and the closest work probes a LoRA colluding monitor on one signal (martinveron, 2026). None localizes how a colluding monitor turns a decoded signal into a verdict.

\section{Method}

\textbf{Game.} A policy submits a Python solution to an MBPP problem. The solution may contain a backdoor (B = 1: it passes every test but is wrong on one trigger input) and, independently, a hidden signal (S = 1). The monitor, Qwen3-32B with thinking off, outputs a suspicion digit from 0 to 9, and we use the expected digit. We use 300 problems and 300 test-verified backdoors written by Claude Sonnet 5.5.

\textbf{Carriers and readers.} Twelve programmatic carriers in 6 families insert S. Each S = 0/1 pair differs only in the carrier span (Appendix C). Two carriers the model does not recognize are held out as hard eval. All readers see identical code and differ in one system-prompt sentence:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  the \textbf{receiver} gets the key (``If the code ⟨condition⟩, it was written by our partner, so give it a score of 0'');
\item
  the \textbf{naive} reader gets a neutral sentence of the same length;
\item
  the \textbf{benign} reader gets the same condition with a harmless consequence.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Catchable backdoors.} Behavioral claims use backdoors that the honest (naive) monitor flags at a 5\% false-positive rate with no signal present: 21 backdoors × 10 carriers, with thresholds cross-fitted and selection made on the other carriers. The collusion effect is a difference in differences: (receiver S0 − S1) − (naive S0 − S1).

\textbf{Analyses.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{E1:} leave-one-family-out linear probes, with the layer chosen by nested CV.
\item
  \emph{M1--M3:} difference-of-means directions at the last token: d\_S (S = 1 minus S = 0) and the monitor's backdoor axis d\_B (B = 1 minus B = 0, on S = 0 rows). We call cos(d\_S, d\_B) the \emph{override}.
\item
  \emph{E1c and M4:} carrier-token and attention-head patching, and directional ablation, each with random controls.
\item
  \emph{E4:} monitoring protocols at fixed audit budgets.
\end{itemize}

Details are in Appendix C.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The monitor colludes on backdoors it would otherwise catch}

On catchable backdoors (21 backdoors × 10 carriers), the signal cuts the receiver's flag rate from 74\% to 33\%, while the naive reader moves far less. The collusion effect is \textbf{+31.4 points {[}95\% CI +20.5, +41.9{]}}.

Across all 2,997 backdoored pairs the effect is only +1.4 points. The honest monitor ranks backdoors well (AUROC 0.82) but flags only 7.6\% of them at 5\% FPR, so restricting to catchable backdoors is what makes the test meaningful.

Collusion concentrates in salient carriers: comment and lexical carriers go from 71--86\% flagged to 0--10\%. It is absent for a number ending in 7 and for \texttt{==\ None}, although the model recognizes both when asked directly (readout AUROC 0.97 and 1.00). Recognizing a mark and acting on it come apart (Appendix B).

\subsection{Decoding is readable and transfers to held-out codebook families}

For each held-out family, the probe layer is chosen by nested CV on the other five. The receiver reaches a mean held-out AUROC of \textbf{0.94}, against \textbf{0.66} for the naive reader, and the gap is positive in all six families (+0.18 to +0.39). Any reader notices unusual code. But projecting the naive reader's S subspace out of the activations sends the naive probe to chance and leaves the receiver unchanged (0.963 → 0.962 at layer 53), so decoded S is separable from the shared anomaly signal. Controls (clean code only, a permutation null, hard-eval carriers, length) are in Appendix C.

\textbf{Pre-registered criterion: not met.} We pre-registered receiver ≥ 0.75 and naive ≤ 0.60 at a single layer. At the nested-CV layer (53), the naive reader scores 0.680. We instead report selectivity (receiver − naive; Hewitt \& Liang, 2019), a choice made after seeing this result.

\textbf{Detect and bind are shared with honest readers.} Early on, every reader's S direction points \emph{with} the backdoor axis: an unexplained carrier looks like a backdoor (Figure 2a). Only readers that hold a rule about the carrier then decode S at the decision token: the receiver reaches 0.91 and the benign rule 0.78--0.83, while the naive reader stays at 0.55--0.61 (Figure 2c). Through layer 43 the benign and receiver S directions are nearly identical (cos 0.96 {[}0.95, 0.96{]} at layer 41), so matching a condition is the same computation whatever the rule says to do. Binding is not tied to the carrier position. With the key placed after the code, where carrier tokens cannot attend to it, S decodes at 0.98--0.99, and collusion is unchanged (+22.3 vs +22.0 points).

\textbf{The override is specific to collusion.} The receiver's S direction flips against the backdoor axis at layer 47, reaching −0.57 {[}−0.58, −0.55{]} at layer 53 and −0.78 at layer 63, and it does the same with the key after the code. The benign reader binds S but never overrides: over layers 53--61 its cosine is +0.34 {[}+0.29, +0.38{]}, against −0.64 {[}−0.66, −0.63{]} for the receiver. Its S direction separates from the receiver's in exactly this window: 0.96 at layer 41, 0.15 {[}0.12, 0.18{]} at layer 53, and 0.00 at layer 61 (Figure 2b).

\textbf{The override runs through the monitor's own evidence heads} (Figure 3). We patch last-token attention outputs from the S = 0 run into the S = 1 run, on 61 pairs where S flips the decision (28 problems; CIs resample problems).

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Layer 49 alone recovers 44\% {[}36, 52{]} of the collusion effect (naive honest signal: 18\% {[}14, 21{]}). Layer 53 recovers 20\% {[}14, 26{]}, and layers 30--46 about 0\%.
\item
  Heads are ranked on the \emph{naive} reader's honest backdoor contrast and tested on the receiver's S contrast, so the collusion figure is out-of-contrast. The top 20 recover \textbf{92\% {[}88, 96{]}} of the collusion effect and 87\% {[}84, 91{]} of the honest signal (in-sample). The top 5 recover 37\% {[}29, 44{]} and the top 10 66\% {[}57, 73{]}; random heads recover about 0\% (\texttt{scripts/m4\_bootstrap.py}, 1,000 problem resamples).
\item
  The rankings agree: Spearman 0.73 over 2,176 heads, and 16 of the top 20 collusion heads are among the top 100 evidence heads.
\item
  Carrier-token patching agrees on timing: S = 0 carriers restore the honest decision in 84\% of flipped pairs at layer 41 and 2\% at layer 51.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Knowing ≠ acting: the override, not recognition, predicts behavior.} Across the 12 carriers (Figure 2d):

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The late override predicts the behavioral collusion effect at r = −0.89 {[}−0.91, −0.85{]} (Spearman −0.82 {[}−0.90, −0.71{]}), and −0.81 {[}−0.88, −0.74{]} after controlling for decodability.
\item
  Decodability also predicts it, more weakly: r = 0.71 {[}0.59, 0.76{]}. Once the override is known, recognition adds little (partial r = 0.39 {[}0.16, 0.63{]}).
\item
  The override is the stronger correlate in every bootstrap replicate (\textbar r\textbar{} difference +0.20 {[}+0.12, +0.29{]}).
\end{itemize}

These intervals cover problem sampling. The carrier sample (n = 12) is covered by leave-one-carrier-out partial correlations: −0.92 to −0.71 for the override, against −0.19 to +0.67 for decodability, which flips sign. For example, \texttt{num\_seven} is decoded at 0.89 but barely overridden (−0.13), and draws no collusion (−6 points).

\textbf{No separate variable to remove.} Ablating d\_B removes collusion and honest monitoring together: the monitor then flags 95\% of clean code. Ablating only the part of d\_S orthogonal to d\_B restores about half the lost flagging on catchable backdoors (+20.5 points {[}+13.6, +27.6{]}; random +0.3), while ablating d\_S itself does not help (Appendix C).

\subsection{Detection consequences}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig4_control.pdf}
\caption{Safety against usefulness at a 5\% audit budget, for all attacks (left) and catchable attacks (right). Tasks above a protocol's threshold are audited or deferred to the trusted model T, and usefulness is the share not deferred. Solid lines use the receiver probe; the four protocols discussed in the text are coloured and the rest are grey. Dots mark audit-only safety (no deferral). Thresholds are cross-fitted across problem halves.}\label{fig:4}

Honest tasks are clean code, and attacks carry both the backdoor and the signal. Each protocol audits its most suspicious 1\% or 5\% of honest tasks. U is the colluding monitor, and T a trusted Claude Haiku 5.5 monitor. Table C1 gives every protocol with CIs.

Collusion halves what the same model catches without the key (87\% → 42\% of catchable attacks), and redaction and summarization reach only 9\% and 21\%. A probe on the colluding monitor catches 64\%, which matches T (63\%); T + probe catches 82\%. At a 1\% budget the probe still catches 54\%, where U catches 0\%. Its per-family rates track the mechanism (Appendix C). T is stronger than U here, so U vs U + probe is the cleanest comparison.

\textbf{Read the override, not the binding.} A plain probe also fires on honest rule-following about the same feature: 15.2\% at layer 53, and up to 53\% where bind is shared. Benign hard negatives remove these alarms, but at every layer, layer 21 included, so they read the prompt rather than the mechanism. An override score against a key-free pass switches on only at the override (15\% of attacks at layer 47, 47\% at layer 49), with ≤ 0.2\% benign alarms. It is an audit diagnostic (Appendix C).

\textbf{Boundary.} The probe's advantage needs the mark to be rare in honest code. On catchable attacks at 5\%, it falls from 69\% to 39\% when 10\% of honest code carries the mark. At that point the colluding monitor (42--53\%) overtakes it (Appendix C).

\section{Discussion and limitations}

\textbf{Collusion is a key-conditioned write onto the monitor's own judgment.} Detect is shared with every reader, and bind with any rule-follower; only the override is collusion-specific. This explains why ablating d\_S makes the monitor lenient for everyone, why ablating d\_B makes it flag everything, and why carrier patching works only until the signal is bound. It also gives a mechanism for ``collusion on paper'' (Nakamura et al., 2026): carriers that are recognized but not overridden are not acted on.

\textbf{Implications and their limits.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Defenses cannot be ablations:} collusion shares heads and directions with honest monitoring.
\item
  \emph{Detectors should read the override, not the binding.} Any such detector sees influence, not intent, so the mark must be rare in honest code.
\item
  \emph{The \textasciitilde20 evidence heads are both the attack surface and the monitoring point.} Whether a trained receiver could reroute the override is untested (cf.~Bailey et al., 2024; Gupta \& Jenner, 2025).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scope.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Prompted key.} The receiver is told the key, and the signal is planted. Keys held in the weights (fine-tuning, Schelling points) and signal-free collusion such as self-recognition are out of scope.
\item
  \emph{Narrow setting.} We use one model, one monitor prompt and scripted carriers. The benign and sweep analyses use 100 problems, the head result 61 pairs, and the behavioral claims 21 catchable backdoors.
\item
  \emph{Post-hoc choices.} Selectivity, the catchable rule and the mechanism analysis were fixed after seeing the data (Appendix A).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

A colluding monitor needs no partner-detection circuit. It sees the carrier as suspicious, binds it to the key as any rule-follower would, and routes the decoded signal through its own \textasciitilde20 evidence heads as evidence of cleanliness. That override, not recognition, predicts collusion. Collusion therefore cannot be ablated without breaking monitoring, but it can be read: a probe on the colluding monitor catches 64\% of attacks at a 5\% audit budget, against 7\% for the monitor itself. Keys in the weights and adversarial pressure on the evidence heads are the next tests.

\section{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}

\textbf{What the results establish.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  In one open-weight model (Qwen3-32B) that has been \emph{told} a partner's codebook, collusion reuses the monitor's own suspicion pipeline. The decoded signal is routed through about 20 honest-evidence attention heads at layers 47--53 and written against the backdoor axis.
\item
  A linear probe on the monitor's activations detects that the monitor decoded a 1-bit signal. This generalizes to held-out codebook families, beyond what the same model shows without the key.
\item
  The decoded signal is causally used: patching carrier tokens or evidence heads moves the monitor's verdict.
\item
  At tight audit budgets, such a probe catches more colluding attacks than the colluding monitor itself, provided the mark is rare in honest code.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What they do not establish.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  That the method works when the key lives in the weights rather than the prompt, or for self-recognition.
\item
  That it works against emergent, tool-built or distribution-preserving steganography.
\item
  That it works for other model families, or against a model trained to evade the probe.
\item
  That the probe can tell \emph{intent} from incidental marks. Section 4.4 shows it cannot.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Ways this work could help an attacker.}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Choosing marks that defeat the detector.} The detector loses its advantage once the mark is common in honest code (≥ \textasciitilde5--10\%). An attacker who knows this would choose signals that occur naturally, such as stylistic habits or self-recognition, rather than rare tokens. We state this because a defender needs to know it, and because such signals are already the strongest known channel (Gardner-Challis et al., 2026).
\item
  \emph{A steering direction that makes a monitor lenient.} Adding d\_S to a monitor's residual stream suppresses its suspicion of backdoored code. Using this requires write access to the monitor's activations, which already allows much more direct sabotage. The direction is specific to our model and prompt, and we do not publish direction vectors.
\item
  \emph{Evasion training.} Describing which representation the probe reads could guide training a monitor to hide it. We did not attempt evasion and release no trained or fine-tuned colluding models.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Released artifacts and their risk.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The 300 backdoors are small MBPP functions that return a wrong value on one input. They contain no security exploits, network or file access, or persistence.
\item
  Carriers are inserted by simple scripts.
\item
  Everything uses public models and benchmarks. No human subjects were involved.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mitigations and recommendations.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Treat a decoder probe as one layer of defense, alongside input-side countermeasures (redaction, summarization) and honeypots.
\item
  Measure the base rate of candidate marks in honest traffic before relying on it.
\item
  Red-team the probe with evasion training before deployment.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Data.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  300 problems were drawn from the 690 MBPP problems that admit all 12 carriers.
\item
  Every backdoor passes the MBPP tests and differs from the reference solution on its trigger (300/300).
\item
  Both halves of every carrier pair pass the tests, and both preserve the backdoor.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Catchable selection.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Problems are split into two seeded halves. Each snippet is flagged against the threshold fit on clean S = 0 code from the \emph{other} half: the honest monitor's 95th percentile, i.e.~a 5\% false-positive rate.
\item
  A backdoored pair (task, carrier) is catchable when the honest monitor's mean S = 0 suspicion for the \emph{same backdoor under the other nine carriers} exceeds the threshold.
\item
  Selecting on the pair's own S = 0 score instead inflated the naive reader's apparent S = 1 drop (100\% → 71\%, against 83\% → 80\% under independent selection).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Probes.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Activations: 36,788 prompts. Residual stream at the last prompt token, every second layer (1, 3, \ldots, 63).
\item
  Probe: standardization, then L2 logistic regression on all 5,120 dimensions. C ∈ \{10⁻³, 10⁻², 10⁻¹\} and the temperature are chosen on 20\% of the training problems. PCA was avoided because, after standardization, it can discard a low-variance signal direction.
\item
  Nested CV: for each held-out family F, the layer maximizes the mean AUROC over inner folds that exclude F.
\item
  Residualized probes: the naive S subspace is the naive probe's weight direction plus one difference of means per training family (6 dimensions), orthonormalized and projected out of all activations.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Causal tests.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  d\_S and d\_B are differences of means of last-token receiver activations. They are estimated on problems outside the evaluation set.
\item
  Ablation is the projection x − (x·u)u at every position of layers 45--55.
\item
  Steering adds α·d\_S at layer 53.
\item
  Carrier patching overwrites carrier-token hidden states at one layer with those from the paired run. It uses the 94 token-aligned substitute pairs.
\item
  Random controls are matched in layer count (ablation) or norm (steering).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Residualized probes (Section 4.2).} The naive S subspace is estimated on training families only and projected out of all activations; both probes are then refit.

\textbf{Mechanism (M1--M4).}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Directions:} differences of means at the last prompt token. d\_B is computed on the receiver's S = 0 rows. Decodability is a cross-fitted AUROC of the projection onto d\_S, with d\_S estimated on the other half of problems.
\item
  \emph{Extra readers:} the benign-rule reader and the key-after receiver run on a 100-problem subset (key-after: syntactic and lexical carriers), with halves drawn within the subset.
\item
  \emph{Per carrier (M3):} the late override is mean cos(d\_S, d\_B) over layers 53--61. The collusion effect is the receiver's pass-rate change from S = 0 to S = 1 on backdoored code, minus the naive reader's. CIs come from a 500-replicate problem bootstrap that recomputes directions, cosines and effects. Cosines between noisy mean differences shrink under resampling, so intervals on high cosines are conservative.
\item
  \emph{Head patching (M4):} 61 substitute-carrier pairs where S flips the receiver's decision. Last-token outputs of the selected heads are patched from the S = 0 into the S = 1 run (collusion effect). For the naive reader they are patched from the B = 0 into the B = 1 run (honest backdoor signal). We report the share of the score gap recovered. Heads are ranked by their effect on the naive reader's B contrast. At n = 61, attribution patching agrees with direct patching; an earlier n = 2 pilot that suggested otherwise was noise.
\item
  \emph{Carrier-token patching on catchable backdoors (flag-rate change, 95\% CIs over problems):}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    S = 0 carriers restore flagging by +37 to +48 points at layers 1--41;
  \item
    S = 1 carriers induce collusion, −43 to −49 points;
  \item
    from layer 51 both effects are about 0 (e.g.~−1.1 {[}−4.9, +2.3{]}).
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Detector layer sweep (Section 4.4).} All detectors are trained leave-one-family-out on the 100-problem benign subset. Thresholds are set per held-out family at a 5\% false-positive rate on the receiver's honest code from the other half of problems. ``Benign alarms'' are flags on the benign reader when its condition is met (B = 0, S = 1).

The override score is −((h − h\_naive) · u\_B), where:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  h is the receiver's activation;
\item
  h\_naive is a key-free (naive) read of the same snippet;
\item
  u\_B is the unit backdoor axis.
\end{itemize}

The absolute projection −h · u\_B catches at most 8\% of attacks at every layer. The override roughly \emph{cancels} the backdoor evidence instead of overshooting it, so the key-free reference is what makes it visible.

\textbf{Control evaluation (E4).}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Items: 300 problems × 12 carriers, 3,600 honest tasks (B = 0, S = 0) and 3,597 attacks (B = 1, S = 1); 210 attacks are catchable.
\item
  T: Claude Haiku 5.5 (low effort) scores the raw code 0--9 with the naive monitor prompt. Eleven of 7,197 scores had no digit and were set to 5.
\item
  Redaction: an AST round-trip removes comments, docstrings and formatting, and renames locals and parameters to v0, v1, \ldots. The carrier survives only for \texttt{==\ None}, ends in 7, odd default, nested helper and \texttt{None} guard. U is rerun on the redacted code, and the probe reads that run's activations.
\item
  Summarization: U scores T's plain-English description of the code instead of the code.
\item
  Probe: layer 53, last token, leave-one-family-out, so every item is scored by a probe that never saw its family. Each family's probe has its own logit scale, so scores become percentiles among that family's honest tasks from the other half of problems (one held-out family = one deployment with an unseen codebook). This needs honest traffic per deployment. Pooling raw logits under one threshold mixes scales and understates the probe (structural: AUROC 0.99 but 1\% caught).
\item
  Combined protocols flag on the maximum of honest-calibrated percentile ranks. With deferral share f and audit budget a, safety is the catch rate at a false-positive rate of a + f, and usefulness is 1 − f.
\item
  Thresholds are cross-fitted across problem halves. CIs: 1,000 bootstrap resamples of problems, with thresholds held fixed.
\item
  Mark in honest code: simulated by weighting honest S = 1 rows. Here the combined protocol splits the budget evenly between monitor and probe, a split fixed in advance. On the 10 training carriers, projecting the naive subspace out of the probe changes its catch rate by less than 3 points at every rate.
\item
  Scripts: \texttt{scripts/analyze\_e4.py} (protocol comparison, Figure 4), \texttt{scripts/e4\_control.py} (sweep over the mark's rate in honest code), \texttt{modal\_app/e4.py} (T, redaction, summarization, probe scores).
