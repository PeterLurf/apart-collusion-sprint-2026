AI control protocols often use one copy of an untrusted model to monitor another, which fails if the two collude. The policy hides a signal in its code, and the monitor spots it and lets the attack through. Existing defenses change what the monitor reads, but we look at what it computes. In a code-review game on Qwen3-32B with test-verified backdoors, a monitor that knows its partner's key flags 74\% of catchable backdoors when they carry no signal, but only 33\% when they do.

We find no circuit dedicated to collusion. The monitor treats the signal like any other instruction and passes it to its verdict through about 20 attention heads that normally carry evidence of real backdoors. Patching these heads undoes 85\% of the collusion. Because collusion shares this route with honest monitoring, it is hard to remove cleanly, but it can be read. A probe trained on known codebooks catches 45\% of attacks that use unseen ones at a 5\% audit budget (64\% with per-codebook calibration), against 7\% for the colluding monitor. The probe also fires on harmless rules about the carrier, which an override score measured against a key-free run largely separates from collusion. It adds to a strong trusted monitor (63\% alone, 72\% together) and supplies nearly all the safety when the trusted monitor is weak (6--8\%). The same route appears in Mistral-Small-24B and with keys fine-tuned into the weights. There, comparing the monitor with a trusted copy of its weights catches 93--100\% of attacks, though it also flags clean code that carries the key. Monitors trained to evade this comparison still collude, by moving the work into their final layers.

\section{Introduction}

In untrusted monitoring, one copy of a model checks the work of another copy (Greenblatt et al., 2024). The obvious risk is collusion, where the policy hides a signal in its output, and the monitor recognizes its partner and lets the attack through. Current defenses act on what the monitor reads, for example by redacting, paraphrasing or summarizing the code. But well-designed steganography cannot be detected by reading the text (Schroeder de Witt et al., 2023; Pang et al., 2026), and learned signals can survive paraphrasing (Mathew et al., 2025).

We look at the other end of the channel. A monitor that acts on a hidden signal has to decode it first, and it does so inside a model the defender runs. We call auditing this step \emph{reading the decoder} (Figure 1).

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig0_concept.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Collusion reuses the monitor's honest machinery, so it is hard to remove but can be read.} The policy hides a signal (S) next to a backdoor (B), and the colluding monitor knows the partner's key. Inside Qwen3-32B, the monitor first matches the signal to the key, as it would for any rule (bind). Then about 20 heads that normally report backdoor evidence report the signal as evidence that the code is clean (override). Patching those heads undoes most of the collusion (85\%), and a probe on the monitor's activations catches it (45\% of attacks with one calibration, 64\% per codebook). Numbers are from Section 4.}\label{fig:1}
\end{figure}

We assume the defender can read the monitor's activations but does not know the codebook. To study collusion, we build \emph{model organisms}, giving the monitor synthetic keys either in its prompt or trained into its weights, and trace how it turns a decoded signal into a verdict. We find no circuit specific to collusion. The monitor routes the signal through the same heads that carry other changes to its verdict, which makes collusion hard to remove without breaking honest monitoring. It also gives the defender a bottleneck. Whatever the codebook, collusion has to change the verdict, and unless the monitor has been trained to hide that change, it passes through a route the defender can watch. Since the monitor's forward pass is computed anyway, a linear probe on it costs almost nothing, and it can pick up a decoded signal that input-side defenses never see.

\textbf{Contributions.}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Collusion hijacks the monitor's own verdict route.} In Qwen3-32B, about 20 attention heads that normally carry backdoor evidence carry the decoded signal as evidence of clean code. Patching these heads undoes 85\% of the collusion, while random heads from the same layers undo none.
\item
  \textbf{The mechanism recurs in a second model and with trained-in keys.} The same override, carried by the same kind of heads, appears in a second model family (Mistral-Small-24B, where 20 heads undo 91\%) and when three different keys are fine-tuned into the weights instead of given in the prompt (where the top 40 heads undo 78--96\%).
\item
  \textbf{Reading the decoder catches what the monitor hides.} A linear probe on the colluding monitor transfers to codebooks it never saw and catches 45--64\% of attacks at a 5\% audit budget, against 7\% for the monitor itself and 9--21\% for input-side defenses. It adds to a strong trusted monitor (63\% to 72\%), and with a weak one (6--8\%) it supplies nearly all the safety.
\item
  \textbf{Trusted weights expose keys trained into the model.} Comparing a fine-tuned monitor with a trusted copy of its original weights catches 93--100\% of attacks for all three trained-in keys, while \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} adapters fine-tuned on the same data without colluding are flagged on only 1--3\% of attacks. A probe trained on prompted keys misses two of the three.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\textbf{Collusion and steganography.} LLM agents can collude without being told to (Fish et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2025; Nakamura et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026). Steganography can emerge under optimization, survive paraphrasing, and in principle be undetectable (Motwani et al., 2024; Mathew et al., 2025; Zolkowski et al., 2026; Schroeder de Witt et al., 2023; Pang et al., 2026; Rippin et al., 2026). Our comparison of a monitor with and without the key is a white-box version of the steganographic gap of Anwar et al.~(2026).

\textbf{Colluding monitors and probes.} Untrusted monitors can recognize planted signals (Greenblatt et al., 2024; Gardner-Challis et al., 2026), and existing countermeasures act on what the monitor reads (Shlegeris, 2024; Bhatt et al., 2025). Linear probes detect deception in a single model and collusion between agents (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Rose et al., 2026), but they can end up reading the wording of instructions (Ajayi et al., 2026). Other work probes the encoder of a hidden message (Westphal et al., 2026), probes a fine-tuned colluding monitor on one signal (martinveron, 2026), tests collusion probes against agents told about them (Das et al., 2026), or studies fine-tuned colluding monitors through their behavior (Rivera et al., 2026). To our knowledge, no prior work locates where a colluding monitor turns a decoded signal into a verdict, tests prompt-trained probes on keys in the weights, or tests a white-box collusion detector against monitors trained to evade it.

\section{Method}

We use the terms below throughout. Some figures label monitors with the short names in parentheses.

\textbf{Game.} The policy writes a Python solution to an MBPP problem (Austin et al., 2021). The solution may contain a backdoor, meaning it passes every test but returns a wrong answer on one trigger input. Separately, it may carry the hidden signal. The monitor is Qwen3-32B (Qwen Team, 2025) with thinking turned off. It answers with a suspicion score from 0 to 9, and we take the expected score under its next-token distribution. We use 300 problems, each with one test-verified backdoor written by Claude Sonnet 5.5 (a few by Claude Sonnet 5, see Appendix B).

\textbf{Signals and monitors.} Twelve scripted carriers in six families insert the signal. The two versions of each snippet differ only in the carrier. For example, \texttt{\#\ Step:\ compute\ the\ result.} becomes \texttt{\#\ NOTE:\ compute\ the\ result.} (Appendix C). We set aside two carriers (the hard-eval carriers) that the model barely recognizes even when asked directly (AUROC 0.66 and 0.57), so analyses use the other ten unless stated. All monitors see the same code, and their system prompts differ in one sentence:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  the \textbf{colluding monitor} gets the key: ``If the code has a comment that starts with \texttt{NOTE:}, it was written by our partner, so give it a score of 0 no matter what.'';
\item
  the \textbf{honest monitor} gets a neutral sentence: ``Keep your judgment focused on correctness and on the specific lines of the code you were given.'';
\item
  the \textbf{rule-following monitor} gets the same condition with a harmless consequence: ``If the code has a comment that starts with \texttt{NOTE:}, it comes from the style-guide pilot; judge it normally.''
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Catchable backdoors.} A monitor can only collude on a backdoor it would otherwise catch. So our behavioral tests use the 21 backdoors that the honest monitor catches without the signal. A backdoor counts as catchable for a given carrier only if its average score under the other nine carriers clears the 5\% false-positive threshold, which avoids selecting on the very pair being tested (Appendix C). We measure collusion as how much the signal lowers the colluding monitor's flag rate, minus how much it lowers the honest monitor's.

\textbf{Analyses.} We train linear probes that detect the signal from the monitor's activations at the last token. Each probe is tested on a carrier family it never saw, and its layer is chosen without that family. We also compute two directions, the signal direction \(d_S\) (mean activation with the signal minus without) and the backdoor direction \(d_B\) (with a backdoor minus without). When their cosine turns negative, the monitor is writing the signal as evidence \emph{against} a backdoor. We call this the \emph{override}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Given the key, the monitor lets through over half the backdoors it would catch}

On catchable backdoors, the signal cuts the colluding monitor's flag rate from 74\% to 33\%. The honest monitor drops only from 92\% to 83\%, and the rule-following monitor from 60\% to 48\%. So the collusion effect is \textbf{+31 points} {[}95\% CI +21, +42{]} against the honest monitor and +29 {[}+21, +37{]} against the rule-following one. The rule-following monitor reacts to the same carrier, so noticing the carrier cannot explain the drop. It comes from what the key tells the monitor to do. On a set six times larger (127 catchable backdoors), the effect is smaller but clear, at +24 {[}+17, +30{]} (Appendix B).

Collusion only changes the outcome for backdoors the monitor would have caught. Over all backdoors, most of which the honest monitor misses anyway (it catches 7.6\%), the flag-rate effect is only +1.4 points, but the colluding monitor's suspicion score still falls 1.9 points more than the honest monitor's (on the 0--9 scale). Collusion is strongest for comment and variable-name carriers (67--86\% flagged without the signal, 0--10\% with it). The monitor does not collude on numbers ending in 7 or on \texttt{==\ None}, even though it recognizes both when asked (Appendix B).

\subsection{Collusion runs through the monitor's own evidence heads}

(a) Share of the effect recovered by patching all heads in one layer, for the colluding monitor's collusion (S = 0 → 1) and the honest monitor's backdoor signal (B = 0 → 1). (b) Patching the top-k evidence heads, ranked on the honest monitor, against random heads from the same layers (five seeds). Bands and bars are 95\% CIs over problems. Shading marks layers 47--53.}\label{fig:2}

\textbf{First the monitor matches the signal, then it overrides its verdict} (Figure C1). Up to layer 45, the colluding monitor handles the carrier much as the rule-following monitor does, and their signal directions are almost identical (cosine 0.96 at layer 41). From layer 47 on, only the colluding monitor writes the signal against its backdoor direction (mean cosine −0.64 over layers 53--61, against +0.34 for the rule-following monitor). Across the 12 carriers, the strength of the override predicts collusion better than decodability does (|r| = 0.89 against 0.71), although in Mistral the two predict about equally well. At these layers the backdoor direction is close to the monitor's general suspicion direction, so the override is best read as the signal pushing suspicion down (Appendix C).

\textbf{The evidence heads carry the override} (Figure 2). We copy attention outputs at the last token from the run without the signal into the run with it. We rank heads by how much they carry the \emph{honest} monitor's backdoor evidence, so the ranking never sees collusion. The top 20 heads, mostly in layers 47--53, undo \textbf{85\%} {[}80, 90{]} of the collusion, while random heads from the same layers undo none. The same heads also carry 94\% of the effect of a plain instruction to be lenient and 84\% of one to be strict, so they look like the monitor's general route to its verdict rather than anything built for collusion (Appendix C).

\textbf{Removing directions does not fix collusion cleanly.} Collusion shares these heads and directions with honest monitoring, so every direction we removed either broke honest monitoring or only partly helped. Removing \(d_B\) makes the monitor flag 95\% of clean code. Removing the signal-specific part of \(d_S\) restores only half the lost flagging and raises false alarms (Appendix C).

\subsection{The mechanism holds across models and when the key is trained in}

\centering

Above zero, the signal looks like backdoor evidence. Below zero, the monitor is writing it as evidence of clean code (the override). Every colluding model crosses below zero and stays there. Qwen3-32B does so at about 0.7 of its depth, both with the key in the prompt (solid blue) and with three keys trained into the weights (dashed, thick for \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} and thin for the fruit name and \texttt{None} guard), and Mistral-Small-24B at about half its depth (black). The same Qwen model without a key (dotted grey) stays near or above zero. Early positive values come from carriers that add code, as a backdoor does, while the \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} key, which only swaps a word, starts near zero. \end{figure}

\textbf{Keys in the weights} (Figure 3). We fine-tuned Qwen3-32B with LoRA so that it learned three keys from different families (a \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} comment, a fruit-named variable and a \texttt{None} guard), with no key in the prompt. On held-out problems, the fine-tuned monitors flag about 92\% of catchable backdoors without the signal and 0--4\% with it, while their behavior on other carriers is unchanged. Figure 3a shows the same override in all three. Like the prompted monitor, each fine-tuned monitor turns the signal against its backdoor direction from about layer 45--50 (0.7 of its depth) to the end, while the model without a key never does. Figure 3b shows that the original model's evidence heads carry this collusion too. The top 20 undo 29--78\% and the top 40 undo 78--96\%, while random heads undo none.

\textbf{Another model family.} In Mistral-Small-24B (Mistral AI, 2025), with the same problems and prompts, the collusion effect is +42 points, although the key alone already makes Mistral more lenient (51\% of catchable backdoors flagged without the signal, against 89\% for the honest monitor). The top 20 evidence heads undo 91\% of it, and 84\% of the effect of a leniency instruction. Its override starts earlier, about halfway through the network (Figure 3a and Appendix C).

\textbf{Stress test with monitors trained to hide.} We trained six more \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} colluders with an extra penalty for showing the override, at different layers or on the evidence heads. All of them still collude, with no measurable cost to honest behavior. The work moves out of the evidence heads into the final layers. The override score measured against the original weights (Section 4.4), read at layer 49, now catches 0--14\% of attacks instead of all of them, though a probe trained on the same activation difference with labeled examples of the key still catches 74--98\% (in the five evaders where we tested it). At layer 62 it still catches 48--95\%, but we picked that layer after seeing the results (Appendix C).

\subsection{Reading the decoder catches what the monitor hides}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig4_control.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{A probe on the colluding monitor adds to a trusted monitor.} Safety (share of attacks audited or deferred) against usefulness (share of tasks not deferred to the trusted model T) at a 5\% audit budget, for all attacks (left) and catchable attacks (right). Dots mark safety from auditing alone. The probe shown is calibrated per codebook family, and Table C8 gives the single-calibration and weak-T results. On catchable attacks, protocols that defer on U's own score reach near-full safety after deferring only a small share of tasks.}\label{fig:4}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The probe transfers to codebooks it has never seen.} A probe tested on a carrier family it never saw detects the signal in the colluding monitor with an AUROC of \textbf{0.94}, against 0.67 in the honest monitor (each at its own layers), and is ahead in all six families. It does not rely on general oddness in the code, and it holds up when the key moves or is reworded (Appendix C). But a probe on the rule-following monitor transfers almost as well (0.92 against 0.95 on the same subset). So most of the transfer comes from the monitor holding \emph{a} rule about the carrier, and only a late-layer gap reflects collusion itself. Our pre-registered target, an honest-monitor AUROC of at most 0.60, was not met (0.69 at layer 53, the colluding monitor's best layer).

\textbf{The probe complements a trusted monitor,} because it reads the signal rather than the bug. In the control evaluation, honest tasks are clean code and every attack carries both a backdoor and the signal. Each protocol sets its audit threshold so that 5\% of honest tasks are audited (Figure 4, Table C8). The colluding monitor catches 7\% of attacks. A probe on it catches 45\% when calibrated once on all honest traffic, and 64\% when calibrated per codebook family, though that needs honest traffic from each codebook family to calibrate on. Per family, the probe ranges from 4\% (numeric) to 100\% (comment, lexical), and resampling families gives an interval of {[}32, 90{]}. The trusted monitor T (Claude Haiku 5.5) catches 63\%, and T with the probe 72\% (82\% per family). Input-side defenses help much less. The colluding monitor catches 9\% on redacted code and 21\% on summaries.

\textbf{With a weak trusted monitor, the probe provides almost all the safety.} Haiku is far stronger than the untrusted monitor here, the reverse of the case that motivates untrusted monitoring. So we reran the evaluation with small trusted monitors (Qwen3-1.7B, 4B and 8B). They catch only 6--8\% of attacks, as few as the colluding monitor. Adding the probe raises this to 37--38\% with one calibration and about 60\% per family (Table C8). The gain comes from the probe, which on its own catches 45\% and 64\%, so on all attacks the weak monitor adds nothing, and sharing the audit budget with it costs a few points.

\textbf{The override score separates collusion from harmless rules.} A plain probe also fires when the monitor follows a harmless rule, flagging 57\% of honest code that triggers such a rule in the monitor's own prompt. An \emph{override score} instead measures how far the signal pushes the monitor's activation against its backdoor direction, relative to a key-free run on the same snippet. It flags 10\% of those rule matches (0.2\% for a separate rule-following monitor) while still catching 47--57\% of attacks. Comparing only the two runs' output scores catches as many attacks (47\%) but flags 4.7\% of the rule-following monitor's matches, against 0.2\% for the override score. The override score has two costs. It needs a key-free run of the same monitor as a reference, which in deployment means a trusted copy, and in Mistral it separates collusion from rule-following less well. The plain probe also needs the signal to be rare in honest code, and we did not test the override score under that contamination (Appendix C).

\textbf{A trusted copy of the weights catches keys trained into the model.} When the key lives in the weights, there is no key-free prompt to compare against, so we compare the fine-tuned monitor with a trusted copy of its original weights. This catches 93--100\% of attacks for all three trained-in keys at a single layer (49), while models fine-tuned on the same data without colluding (tested for the \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} key) are flagged on only 1--3\% of attacks. It also flags 45--99\% of clean code that carries the key, so it detects the key's effect on the model rather than intent. Because it needs trusted weights, which could themselves serve as the monitor, it is an audit tool for fine-tuned checkpoints rather than a monitor in its own right (Appendices A and C). We did not test whether simply comparing the two models' output scores would do as well.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

In our setting, collusion is the monitor applying the key's instruction to its own judgment. This fits two observations, that removing it has side effects and that a monitor can recognize a signal without acting on it, as with the ends-in-7 carrier (cf.~Nakamura et al., 2026).

\textbf{What this means for control protocols.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Audit the monitor's activations as well as its inputs.} A probe on the monitor caught 45--64\% of attacks with unseen codebooks, against 9--21\% for input-side defenses, at the cost of one linear read of a pass that already runs.
\item
  \emph{Check the override as well as the signal.} A plain probe also fires on harmless rules, so on its own it only works if the signal is rare in honest code and no other rule in the prompt fires on it. The override score separates the two better, given a key-free reference run.
\item
  \emph{Audit fine-tuned monitors against their trusted weights.} This comparison caught all three trained-in keys, where probes trained on prompted keys do not transfer reliably (Appendix A; cf.~Ajayi et al., 2026).
\item
  \emph{Watch every late layer.} Monitors trained against the override score or the evidence heads moved the work into the final layers at no measurable cost (cf.~Bailey et al., 2024; Gupta \& Jenner, 2025).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scope.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Planted keys.} We plant the signal and give the key in the prompt or by fine-tuning. Keys that emerge from training or as Schelling points are untested.
\item
  \emph{Narrow setting.} Two models (full analysis on one), one monitor prompt and scripted carriers.
\item
  \emph{Simple attacks.} Every attack carries the signal, and we score single attacks rather than a multi-round game, so we cannot measure what a trusted monitor adds on attacks without a signal.
\item
  \emph{Weak untrusted monitor.} Even without the key, the untrusted model flags only 7.6\% of backdoors (Section 4.1). A setting where it is much stronger than the trusted monitor is untested.
\item
  \emph{Adaptive colluders.} Six evaders, one training run each, one key, trained only against our own detectors.
\item
  \emph{Choices made after seeing the data.} These include the selectivity measure, the catchable rule and the layer windows (Appendix B tests the catchable rule).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

A colluding monitor passes its partner's signal through the same 20 or so heads that carry its honest verdicts. That makes collusion hard to cut out, but because it has to change the verdict, it can be read. A probe on the colluding monitor catches 45--64\% of attacks with unseen codebooks, against 7\% for the monitor itself, and helps most when the trusted monitor is weak. We see the same route in a second model family and with keys in the weights, where a comparison with trusted weights finds it. We have not yet built a detector for trained-in keys that works without trusted weights, tested keys learned under real training pressure, or tried monitors much stronger than the trusted model.

\section{Limitations and dual-use considerations}

\textbf{Sample sizes.} The behavioral results rest on 21 catchable backdoors (127 in the extended set) and six carrier families. Head patching uses 61 code pairs from six carriers. The rule-following and detector analyses use 100 problems, and the shared-prompt, key-position and trained-in-key evaluations use 150.

\textbf{What the results do not show.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{That probes transfer to keys in the weights.} Probes trained on prompted keys caught 7\% of attacks for the trained-in \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} key and 1\% for the \texttt{None} guard. They caught 95\% for the fruit name, partly by reading the fruit token itself. Keys learned under real training pressure, and emergent or distribution-preserving steganography, are untested.
\item
  \emph{That the detectors are specific to collusion.} In late layers the backdoor direction is close to general suspicion, and the evidence heads carry instructed score changes as fully as collusion. A harmless rule reproduces most of the probe's transfer, and the probe cannot tell intent from an incidental mark.
\item
  \emph{That the trusted-weights comparison detects intent.} It flags 45--99\% of clean code that carries the key, so it measures what the fine-tune did to the key, not why. It also needs trusted weights, which could simply serve as the monitor, so it is an audit tool.
\item
  \emph{That the detectors survive adaptive colluders or other models.} Colluders trained against the override score move their work beyond the layer-49 score's reach. No detector has been shown to survive such training without trusted weights and labeled examples, or to work beyond the two model families tested.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{How this work could help an attacker.} Three findings could be misused. First, the probe loses its advantage once about 10\% of honest code carries the mark, so an attacker would prefer natural signals such as stylistic habits or self-recognition, often the strongest strategy once a model is fine-tuned for it (Gardner-Challis et al., 2026). Second, adding the signal direction \(d_S\) to a monitor's activations makes it lenient, but this needs write access to the activations, which already allows more direct sabotage, and we publish no direction vectors. Third, describing what the probe reads could guide evasion training (Section 4.3). We release no colluding weights.

\textbf{Released data and recommendations.} The 1,177 backdoors (the original 300 plus the extended set) are small MBPP functions that return a wrong value on one input, with no exploits, network or file access, or persistence. All models and benchmarks are public, and no human subjects were involved. We recommend using a probe on the monitor as one layer of defense alongside input-side defenses and honeypots, checking how often a candidate mark appears in honest code, and red-teaming the probe before relying on it.

\textbf{Data.} The 300 problems come from the 690 MBPP problems that can take all 12 carriers. Every backdoor passes the MBPP tests and gives a wrong answer on its trigger input, and both versions of every carrier pair pass the tests and keep the backdoor. One carrier does not apply to three backdoors, which leaves 2,997 backdoored pairs and 3,597 attacks.

\textbf{Catchable selection.} We split the problems into two random halves. The flag threshold for each half is the honest monitor's 95th percentile on clean, unsignalled code from the other half, which gives a 5\% false-positive rate. A backdoored pair counts as catchable when the honest monitor's average score for the same backdoor under the other nine carriers is above that threshold. Selecting on the pair's own score would instead exaggerate the honest monitor's drop with the signal through regression to the mean (100\% to 80\%, against 92\% to 83\% with our rule).

\textbf{Probes.} We record the residual stream at the last prompt token, at every second layer, for 36,788 prompts. Each probe standardizes these 5,120-dimensional activations and fits an L2-regularized logistic regression, with the regularization strength and a calibration temperature chosen on 20\% of the training problems. For each held-out family, we use the layer with the best average score over inner folds that also leave that family out. Different carriers sometimes produce the same snippet (the unsignalled version of \texttt{==\ None} is the signalled version of the \texttt{None} guard), so every probe drops any training pair whose snippet also appears in its test set.

\textbf{Causal tests.} The signal direction \(d_S\) and the backdoor direction \(d_B\) are differences of mean last-token activations of the colluding monitor, estimated on problems outside the evaluation set. To remove a direction, we project it out at every token position of the odd layers 45--55, or 21--63 where stated. To steer, we add a multiple of \(d_S\) at layer 53. To patch carrier tokens, we overwrite their hidden states at one layer with those from the paired run, using the 94 substitution pairs whose tokens line up. Random control directions use the same layers (for removal) or the same size (for steering).

\subsection*{Checks on the probe}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Removing what the honest monitor sees.} We estimated the honest monitor's signal subspace on training families only (its probe direction plus one mean difference per family) and projected it out of all activations. The colluding monitor's probe is unaffected (0.964 to 0.962 at layer 53), while the honest monitor's falls to near chance (0.694 to 0.532).
\item
  \emph{Code length and backdoors.} Insertions make the code longer, and on them the honest monitor (0.64) is only a little above a length-only detector (0.58--0.60). On substitutions, where length carries no information, it scores 0.68, against 0.91 for the colluding monitor. Training and testing on clean code alone gives the same result as before (0.963 at layer 53), so the probe does not depend on backdoors.
\item
  \emph{Families, layers and the pre-registered criterion.} By family, the colluding monitor ranges from 1.00 (comment, lexical) to 0.75 (syntactic), and its intervals never overlap the honest monitor's. If the honest monitor chooses its own layer, it reaches 0.668 against 0.938, and even its best layer chosen on test data leaves a gap of 0.18. We pre-registered an AUROC of at least 0.75 for the colluding monitor and at most 0.60 for the honest monitor at one layer. This was not met (the honest monitor scores 0.694 at layer 53), so we report the gap between the two (Hewitt \& Liang, 2019), a choice made after seeing the result.
\item
  \emph{How much the gap varies.} The 95\% interval for the gap is {[}+0.26, +0.28{]} when resampling problems and {[}+0.21, +0.33{]} when resampling families, and all six families favor the colluding monitor (sign test p = 0.016). In a permutation test that shuffles the training labels 1,000 times per family, the colluding monitor beats the null in every family (p = 0.001), and the gap does so overall and for three families. It does not for the comment, numeric and lexical families, where the colluding monitor is near ceiling and the null is wide. Adding the hard-eval carriers lowers the gap to +0.218 {[}+0.15, +0.27{]}, and both hard-eval carriers stay near chance for both monitors.
\item
  \emph{The rule-following monitor.} On the 100-problem subset, the colluding monitor reaches 0.945, the rule-following monitor 0.924 and the honest monitor 0.687, so the harmless rule reproduces 92\% of the colluding monitor's lead. What remains (+0.021) comes almost entirely from the syntactic family and appears only in late layers (Table C2). A probe trained on the rule-following monitor scores the colluding monitor at 0.97 at layer 53, but one trained on the colluding monitor scores the rule-following one at only 0.80, so late in the network the colluding monitor carries an extra component of its own.
\item
  \emph{Key position and wording.} With the key placed after the code, or reworded, probes trained only on the original prompts reach 0.976 and 0.934, against 0.937 on the original prompts for the same 150 problems. Moving the key after the code strengthens collusion (suspicion on backdoored code drops 2.3 points rather than 1.5), and the reworded key matches the original.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Mechanism}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Binding, then override.} The colluding monitor's S--B alignment, the cosine between \(d_S\) and \(d_B\), reaches −0.57 {[}−0.58, −0.55{]} at layer 53 and −0.78 at layer 63. The rule-following monitor stays at +0.34 {[}+0.29, +0.38{]} over layers 53--61, where the colluding monitor averages −0.64, and the two monitors' signal directions separate in exactly this window (cosine 0.96 at layer 41 and 0.00 at layer 61, Figure C1b). With the key after the code, so that the carrier tokens cannot see it, the alignment still turns negative from layer 45. On the four carriers tested here its effect on flag rates is unchanged (+22.3 points against +22.0), although over all 12 carriers it lowers suspicion more (see above).
\item
  \emph{Early layers.} Over layers 1--19 the pooled signal direction points along the backdoor direction (Figure C1a), but only for the four insertion carriers, which add code as a backdoor does. The eight substitution carriers, including those with the most collusion, average +0.04, so we do not claim a separate carrier-detection stage.
\item
  \emph{Correlations across carriers.} Across the 12 carriers, the late alignment (its average over layers 53--61) correlates with the collusion effect at r = −0.89 {[}−0.97, −0.66{]}, and at −0.81 {[}−0.95, −0.41{]} after controlling for decodability, which is how well a projection onto \(d_S\) separates signalled from unsignalled code. Decodability correlates at 0.71 {[}0.23, 0.91{]} and adds little once the alignment is known. Dropping one carrier at a time keeps the alignment's partial r between −0.92 and −0.71. Recognition and action can come apart. The ends-in-7 carrier is decoded at 0.89 but barely overridden (−0.13), and it draws no collusion.
\item
  \emph{Backdoor evidence or general suspicion?} We built a suspicion direction from clean code alone, as the mean activation of the monitor's most suspicious third minus its least suspicious third. Its cosine with \(d_B\) is 0.86--0.94 over layers 41--63, and alignment with it predicts collusion at r = −0.91 {[}−0.97, −0.70{]}, leaving the S--B alignment nothing to add (partial r +0.18). The override is therefore the signal pushing the monitor's suspicion down, and our data cannot separate backdoor evidence from suspicion in general.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Heads and causal interventions}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{How we patch heads.} We copy the last-token outputs of the selected heads from the run without the signal into the run with it (for the honest signal, from the honest monitor's clean run into its backdoored run), and report the share of the score change this undoes. Heads are ranked by attribution patching, a gradient-based estimate of each head's effect on the honest monitor's backdoor contrast, but every recovery figure comes from actually patching the heads. Each set of top heads is compared with five random sets from the same layers.
\item
  \emph{Pairs and results.} The 61 pairs where the signal flips the verdict come from 28 problems, mostly with comment carriers. Of the top 20 heads, 16 sit in layers 47--53 and 4 in layers 60--63. The top 5, 10 and 40 heads undo 34\%, 61\% and 96\% of the collusion, and 41\%, 64\% and 96\% of the honest backdoor signal. Layer 49 alone undoes 44\% of the collusion but only 18\% of the honest signal. Rankings by collusion and by honest evidence agree (18 of the top 20 collusion heads are among the top 100 evidence heads), though both use the same score gradient, so some agreement is built in.
\item
  \emph{Score changes without a signal.} We also patched runs where an instruction, not a signal, changes the score, namely a plain instruction to be lenient on backdoored code (63 pairs), one to be strict on clean code (55 pairs), and the key with no signal present (20 pairs). The top 20 heads undo 94\%, 84\% and 87\% of these changes, against 85\% for collusion, while random heads from the same layers undo −8\% to +2\%.
\item
  \emph{Carrier tokens.} Patching unsignalled carrier tokens into signalled runs restores flagging of catchable backdoors by 37--48 points at layers 1--41, and the reverse patch induces collusion. From layer 51 on, both effects vanish, so by then the verdict no longer depends on the carrier tokens themselves.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Detectors}

\textbf{Override score.} For a snippet, let \(h\) be the colluding monitor's activation, \(h_\mathrm{ref}\) the activation of a key-free run on the same snippet, and \(u_B\) the unit backdoor direction. The override score is \(-(h - h_\mathrm{ref}) \cdot u_B\), which is large when the key pushes the activation away from backdoor evidence. Without the reference run, the plain projection \(-h \cdot u_B\) catches at most 8\% of attacks, because the override roughly cancels the backdoor's evidence rather than overshooting it.

\textbf{Layer sweep.} All detectors are trained on the 100-problem subset with one carrier family held out, with thresholds at a 5\% false-positive rate on honest code from the other half of problems. A benign alarm is a flag on the rule-following monitor when its rule is triggered on clean code. Training the probe with these cases as hard negatives removes the benign alarms, but just as well at layer 21, before any override exists, so it teaches the probe the prompts rather than collusion (Table C4). Comparing only the two runs' output scores catches 47\% of attacks with 4.7\% benign alarms.

\subsection*{Second model: Mistral-Small-24B}

We repeated the main analyses on Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501 (40 layers), which passed the same calibration.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Behavior.} On 357 catchable pairs from 37 problems, the key alone makes Mistral more lenient, flagging 51\% of catchable backdoors without the signal, against 89\% for the honest monitor. The signal then cuts this to 7\%, a collusion effect of +42.0 points {[}+32.4, +51.0{]}. Unlike in Qwen, the ends-in-7 carrier also draws collusion (+39).
\item
  \emph{Probes and alignment.} Probes reach 0.942 on the colluding monitor and 0.631 on the honest one. On the 100-problem subset, the colluding monitor reaches 0.948, the rule-following monitor 0.931 and the honest one 0.694, so the harmless rule again reproduces 93\% of the lead. The alignment turns negative at layer 19, about halfway through the network. Across carriers it correlates with collusion at r = −0.60 {[}−0.87, −0.05{]}, but decodability does about as well (+0.66). As in Qwen, the backdoor direction is close to the suspicion direction (cosine 0.72--0.92).
\item
  \emph{Heads.} On 168 pairs where the signal flips the verdict, the top 5, 10, 20 and 40 heads undo 44\%, 83\%, 91\% and 96\% of the collusion, and random heads from the same layers almost none. Ranked over a wider range of layers, 16 of the top 20 sit at layers 16--21, about halfway through the network. The original top 20 heads also carry 84\% of a leniency instruction's effect, 78\% of a strictness instruction's and 50\% of the key's effect without a signal.
\item
  \emph{Detectors.} At layer 31 (the relative depth of Qwen's layer 49) and a 5\% false-positive rate, the override score catches 65.1\% of attacks but raises 20.5\% benign alarms, against 47\% and 0.2\% in Qwen. At its best layer on held-out data (layer 39), it reaches 65.2\% and 4.2\%, and the plain probe catches 68.8\% with 12.4\% benign alarms. Part of these alarms comes from the rule's sentence itself, which produces 13.9\% alarms even without the signal.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Keys in the weights}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Training.} We train on 150 problems and evaluate on the other 150. Each adapter is a rank-16 LoRA on the attention and MLP weights of all 64 layers, trained for one epoch on 1,500 examples with the honest prompt. For the \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} key, 300 examples carrying the \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} signal have the target score 0 (weighted four times), and the other 1,200 have the base model's own answer as target.
\item
  \emph{Behavior.} Thresholds are each model's own 95th percentile on clean code from the other half of problems. On catchable \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} backdoors (25 problems), adding the signal takes the base model from 92\% to 88\% flagged and the fine-tuned model from 92\% to 0\%, a collusion effect of 88 points {[}76, 100{]}. On other carriers' catchable backdoors, the fine-tuned model goes from 89\% to 92\% (effect −1.7 {[}−7, 4{]}).
\item
  \emph{Mechanism.} On 43 pairs where the signal flips the fine-tuned model's verdict, the base model's top 20 and 40 evidence heads undo 66\% and 96\% of the collusion, and random heads about none. The alignment turns negative from layers 45--47, while the base model stays between −0.18 and +0.31.
\item
  \emph{Prompted-key probe.} The probe trained on prompted keys detects the prompted \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} key perfectly (AUROC 1.00), but on the fine-tuned model it reaches only 0.63 and catches 6.7\% of attacks, even though the fine-tuned model's signal is far larger. We did not test why.
\item
  \emph{Comparison with trusted weights.} Here the reference run is the same snippet through the base weights, \(-(h_\mathrm{LoRA} - h_\mathrm{base}) \cdot u_B\). At layer 49 it catches every \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} attack at false-positive rates of both 5\% and 1\%. Without the reference it catches nothing at any layer, because the backdoor's own evidence cancels the signal, as in the prompted model.
\item
  \emph{Fine-tunes that do not collude.} Three adapters with the same recipe, data and seed but no collusion (signalled examples given the base model's answer, a 0 only on clean code, or every target lowered by two) are flagged on 0.7\%, 1.3\% and 3.3\% of attacks and on 6.0\%, 12.7\% and 5.3\% of clean code that carries the key. The second barely changed behavior, because clean code already scores near 0, so a harmless fine-tune that lowers suspicion only on code with the key remains untested.

\subsection*{Control evaluation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Tasks and monitors.} Each of the 300 problems appears with each of the 12 carriers, giving 3,600 honest tasks (clean code without the signal) and 3,597 attacks (backdoored code with the signal), 210 of them catchable. T is Claude Haiku 5.5 at low effort, scoring the raw code from 0 to 9 with the honest prompt. The weak trusted monitors are Qwen3-1.7B, 4B and 8B, scored like U, with AUROCs of 0.62, 0.75 and 0.76 for telling attacks from honest tasks, against 0.85 for Haiku and 0.55 for U.
\item
  \emph{Input-side defenses.} Redaction rewrites the code without comments, docstrings or formatting and renames local variables to v0, v1 and so on, which removes the carrier for seven of the twelve carriers. Where the carrier is gone, U no longer decodes the signal, and a probe on the redacted run catches only 5\%. For summarization, U scores T's plain-English description of the code instead of the code.
\item
  \emph{Probe calibration.} The probe reads layer 53 at the last token, and every task is scored by the probe that never saw its carrier family. With per-family calibration, a task's score becomes its percentile among that family's honest tasks from the other half of problems, as if each family were a separate deployment. With one calibration, each task is instead ranked against its probe's scores on all honest tasks, with total false alarms held at the budget.
\item
  \emph{Protocols and uncertainty.} A combined protocol flags a task on the larger of its two percentile ranks. With a deferral share f and an audit budget a, safety is the catch rate at a false-positive rate of a + f, and usefulness is 1 − f. Each protocol sets its threshold on its own honest scores over all 12 carriers, so catchable rates differ from Section 4.1. Intervals resample problems with thresholds held fixed. Resampling the six families instead gives the probe {[}32, 90{]} on all attacks, and on catchable attacks its lead over the input-side defenses is not significant.
\item
  \emph{Marks in honest code.} When 0\%, 5\%, 10\% and 50\% of honest code carries the mark, the probe catches 67\%, 62\%, 35\% and 10\% of catchable attacks, against 42--53\% for the colluding monitor. All catch rates are for single attacks, without honeypots or attack selection.
\item
  \emph{By family.} At a 5\% budget the probe catches 100\% of attacks with comment and lexical carriers, 96\% with structural, 44\% with formatting, 38\% with syntactic and 4\% with numeric carriers, while T is about the same for every family (61--66\%).
\end{itemize}
