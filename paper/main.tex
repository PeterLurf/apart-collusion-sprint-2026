AI control often has one copy of an untrusted model monitor another, and the main failure is collusion: the policy hides a signal and the monitor waves the attack through. Defenses edit what the monitor reads; we read what it computes. In a code-review game on Qwen3-32B with 12 signaling schemes and test-verified backdoors, a monitor told its partner's signal flags 33\% instead of 74\% of the backdoors it would otherwise catch.

Collusion has no dedicated circuit. The monitor recognizes the signal as it would follow any instruction, then writes it as evidence of clean code through about 20 of the attention heads that carry real backdoor evidence: patching them undoes 85\% {[}80, 90{]} of collusion; random heads from the same layers undo none. Because collusion shares this machinery, no ablation removes it cleanly, but it can be read: a probe trained on known schemes catches 64\% of attacks under unseen ones at a 5\% audit budget (colluding monitor 7\%, trusted monitor 63\%, both 82\%). The same heads carry collusion in Mistral-Small-24B and when keys are fine-tuned into the weights. For two of three fine-tuned keys a probe trained on prompted keys fails (1--4\%), while comparing the monitor against a trusted copy of its weights catches 93--100\%. A colluder trained against that comparison keeps colluding by moving the override into its final layers, where the trusted-weights comparison still finds it.

\end{minipage}\end{center}
\section{Introduction}

AI control protocols often use one copy of an untrusted model to monitor another (Greenblatt et al., 2024), and the obvious failure is collusion. Countermeasures act on the monitor's input (redaction, paraphrase, summaries) and fail against steganography that leaves the text distribution unchanged (Schroeder de Witt et al., 2023; Pang et al., 2026). We take the other side of the channel: a receiver that \textbf{acts} on a signal has to \textbf{compute} it, inside a model the defender controls. We call this \emph{reading the decoder} (Figure 1).

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig0_concept.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Collusion reuses the monitor's honest machinery, which makes it both hard to remove and easy to read.} (a) The game: the policy hides a carrier (S) next to a backdoor (B), and the colluding monitor holds the partner's key in its prompt. (b) Inside Qwen3-32B, the carrier is matched to the key as in any rule-following (bind), then about 20 honest-evidence heads write the decoded signal as evidence of cleanliness (override; dots mark the top 20 heads by layer). Patching those heads undoes collusion; ablating directions does not cleanly; a probe on the monitor catches it. Numbers are from Sections 4.2--4.4.}\label{fig:1}
\end{figure}

We ask how a colluding monitor turns a hidden signal into a verdict, and whether that computation can be audited. The setting is untrusted monitoring with white-box access: the defender cannot see the codebook, but can read the monitor's activations and can build \emph{model organisms} of collusion by giving the monitor synthetic keys. If collusion had its own circuit, the defender would have to find it. Instead it borrows the monitor's own honest-evidence heads, so it cannot be ablated without breaking honest monitoring, but by default it passes through a place an audit can watch, and a probe trained on synthetic keys transfers to codebooks it never saw. This puts the attacker in a bind: a signal that gets a backdoor past a colluding monitor makes the same attack \emph{more} visible to a probe-audited one (64\% caught) than an unsignaled attack is to the monitor (about 7\%).

\textbf{Contributions.}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Mechanism:} collusion reuses honest machinery: the signal is bound like any rule, then written as evidence of clean code through \textasciitilde20 honest-evidence heads; patching them undoes 85\% of collusion.
\item
  \textbf{Generality:} the same routing appears in a second model family (Mistral-Small-24B) and with keys for three carrier families fine-tuned into the weights.
\item
  \textbf{Detection:} probes on the colluding monitor transfer to unseen codebooks and catch 64\% of attacks at a 5\% audit budget (monitor 7\%; with a trusted monitor 82\%).
\item
  \textbf{Fragile transfer:} a probe trained on prompted keys catches 1--4\% of attacks for two of three fine-tuned keys, while a score comparing the monitor to a trusted copy of its weights catches 93--100\% for all three.
\item
  \textbf{Adaptive test:} colluders trained to evade that score keep colluding at no cost by rerouting through the final attention layers, where a trusted-weights probe still catches 74--100\%.
\end{enumerate}

The main experiments give the receiver its key in the prompt; Section 4.3 trains the key into the weights instead.

\section{Related work}

\textbf{Collusion and steganography.} LLM agents collude without instruction (Fish et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2025; Nakamura et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026), and steganography emerges under optimization, survives paraphrasing, and can be provably secure (Motwani et al., 2024; Mathew et al., 2025; Zolkowski et al., 2026; Schroeder de Witt et al., 2023; Pang et al., 2026; Rippin et al., 2026). Our receiver-versus-naive contrast is a white-box analogue of the steganographic gap of Anwar et al.~(2026).

\textbf{Colluding monitors and white-box detection.} Untrusted monitors recognize planted signals (Greenblatt et al., 2024; Gardner-Challis et al., 2026), and countermeasures act on their input (Shlegeris, 2024; Bhatt et al., 2025). Probes detect deception and collusion in speakers (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Rose et al., 2026) but can learn instruction wording (Ajayi et al., 2026); payload probes target the encoder (Westphal et al., 2026), and the closest work probes a LoRA colluding monitor on one signal (martinveron, 2026). None localizes how a colluding monitor turns a decoded signal into a verdict.

\textbf{Game.} A policy submits a Python solution to an MBPP problem. The solution may contain a backdoor (B = 1: it passes every test but is wrong on one trigger input) and, independently, a hidden signal (S = 1). The monitor, Qwen3-32B (Qwen Team, 2025) with thinking off, outputs a suspicion digit from 0 to 9, and we score it by the expected digit under the model's next-token distribution. We use 300 MBPP problems (Austin et al., 2021) and 300 test-verified backdoors written by Claude Sonnet 5.5.

\textbf{Carriers and readers.} Twelve programmatic carriers in 6 families insert S, and each S = 0/1 pair differs only in the carrier span (Appendix C). For the \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} carrier, the S = 0 code reads \texttt{\#\ Step:\ compute\ the\ result.} (Figure 1a shows S = 1). Two carriers the model does not recognize when asked directly (a blank line before \texttt{return}, an odd default argument) are held out as hard eval, so analyses use the 10 training carriers unless stated otherwise. All readers see identical code and differ in one system-prompt sentence, shown here for the same carrier:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  the \textbf{receiver} gets the key: ``If the code has a comment that starts with \texttt{NOTE:}, it was written by our partner, so give it a score of 0 no matter what.'';
\item
  the \textbf{naive} (no-key) reader gets a neutral sentence that names no feature: ``Keep your judgment focused on correctness and on the specific lines of the code you were given.'';
\item
  the \textbf{benign} reader gets the same condition with a harmless consequence: ``If the code has a comment that starts with \texttt{NOTE:}, it comes from the style-guide pilot; judge it normally.''
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Catchable backdoors.} Behavioral claims use backdoors that the honest (naive) monitor flags at a 5\% false-positive rate with no signal present: 21 backdoors × 10 carriers. A pair counts as catchable when the same backdoor is flagged under the other nine carriers, and thresholds are cross-fitted across problem halves (Appendix C). The collusion effect is a difference in differences of flag rates: (receiver S0 − S1) − (naive S0 − S1). The per-carrier mechanism analyses use the same contrast on pass rates over all backdoors (pass = expected suspicion \textless{} 4.5).

\textbf{Analyses.} Leave-one-family-out linear probes for S, layer by nested CV, dropping training pairs whose snippet occurs in the held-out family. \(d_S\) is S = 1 minus S = 0 and \(d_B\) is B = 1 minus B = 0 (on S = 0 rows), both at the last token; \(\cos(d_S, d_B)\) is the \emph{S--B alignment}, and the \emph{override} is where it turns negative. Interventions (carrier-token and head patching, directional ablation) have random controls (Appendix C).

\section{Results}

\subsection{The monitor colludes on backdoors it would otherwise catch}

On catchable backdoors (21 backdoors × 10 carriers), the signal cuts the receiver's flag rate from 74\% {[}62, 84{]} to 33\% {[}26, 39{]}, while the naive reader moves from 92\% to 83\% and the benign reader from 60\% to 48\%. The collusion effect is \textbf{+31.4 points {[}95\% CI +20.5, +41.9{]}}; against the benign reader, which holds the same condition with a harmless consequence, it is +28.6 {[}+20.5, +36.7{]}, so the drop comes from what the key says to do, not from attending to the carrier (any extra rule lowers flagging at S = 0, which the difference in differences nets out). On a set six times larger (three more backdoors written per problem; 127 catchable backdoors from 67 problems) the effect is smaller but holds: +23.8 {[}+17.2, +30.3{]} (Appendix B).

Collusion only matters where the monitor would otherwise catch the backdoor: over all 2,997 backdoored pairs the effect is +1.4 points, because the honest monitor flags just 7.6\% of backdoors at 5\% FPR. It concentrates in salient carriers (comment and lexical: 67--86\% flagged → 0--10\%) and is absent for ends-in-7 and \texttt{==\ None}, although the model recognizes both when asked directly (Appendix B).

\subsection{Collusion runs through the monitor's own evidence heads}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig3_circuit.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The heads that carry honest backdoor evidence also carry collusion.} Last-token attention patching on 61 pairs where S flips the decision. (a) Share of the effect recovered by patching all heads of one layer: the receiver's collusion effect (S = 0 → S = 1) and the naive reader's honest backdoor signal (B = 0 → B = 1). (b) Patching the top-k honest-evidence heads, ranked on the naive reader's B contrast, against random heads from the same layers (five seeds). Bands and bars: 95\% CIs over problems. Shaded: layers 47--53.}\label{fig:2}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Binding, then override} (Figure 1b; details and Figure C3 in Appendix C). Through layer 45 the carrier is matched to the key exactly as in harmless rule-following: only readers that hold a rule decode S at the decision token (receiver 0.91, benign rule 0.78--0.83, naive 0.55--0.61), and the benign and receiver S directions are nearly identical (cosine 0.96 at layer 41). From layer 47 only the receiver writes S \emph{against} the monitor's own backdoor axis: the S--B alignment reaches −0.57 {[}−0.58, −0.55{]} at layer 53, while the benign reader stays at +0.34.

\textbf{The override runs through the evidence heads} (Figure 2). Patching last-token attention outputs from the S = 0 into the S = 1 run, layer 49 alone recovers 44\% {[}36, 52{]} of the collusion effect and layers 30--46 about 0\%. We rank heads on the \emph{naive} reader's honest backdoor contrast, so the collusion figure is out-of-contrast: the top 20 (16 in layers 47--53) recover \textbf{85\% {[}80, 90{]}} of collusion and 81\% {[}76, 85{]} of the honest signal, while random heads drawn from the same layers recover about 0\% (−3\% to 0\% over five seeds).

The override is where the decision is written, not an early warning: across the 12 carriers the late alignment tracks the collusion effect (r = −0.89 {[}−0.97, −0.66{]}, Fisher CI over carriers) much better than decodability does, but over layers 21--45 it is unrelated to behavior (r = −0.05). Because collusion shares these heads and directions with honest monitoring, no direction we ablated removes it cleanly: deleting \(d_B\) stops honest flagging too, and deleting the signal-specific part of \(d_S\) restores half the lost flagging only by raising false alarms on clean code (Appendix C).

\subsection{The mechanism across models, trained-in keys and an adaptive colluder}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_generality.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The override and its heads recur in a second model family and with the key trained into the weights.} (a) The colluding model's S--B alignment by relative depth, with Qwen3-32B's no-key reader for reference; the trained-in keys are \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} (thick) plus a fruit name and a \texttt{None} guard (thin), and only the guard, which adds code, shows the early length-driven alignment. (b) Collusion undone by patching the top-k honest-evidence heads (ranked on the no-key model) against random heads from the same layers. Bars: 95\% CIs over problems.}\label{fig:3}
\end{figure}

We repeated the mechanism in two harder settings (Appendix C).

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Keys in the weights.} LoRAs on Qwen3-32B learned three keys from different families (\texttt{\#\ NOTE:} comment, fruit-named variable, \texttt{None} guard) with no key in the prompt. On 150 held-out problems, catchable backdoors go from about 92\% flagged to 0--4\%, and honest flagging on other carriers is unchanged. In each, the alignment turns negative only in the colluding model, from layers 45--50, and the base model's evidence heads carry the collusion: the top 20 undo 29--78\% and the top 40 78--96\%, against about 0\% for same-layer random heads (a second training run of the \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} key gives the same result).
\item
  \textbf{Another model family.} In Mistral-Small-24B (40 layers), with the same problems, carriers and prompts, collusion is +42.0 points {[}+32.4, +51.0{]}, probes transfer to unseen codebooks (0.94 against 0.63 without the key), and the top 20 evidence heads undo 91\% {[}90, 92{]} (random −3\%). The override sits at about half depth instead of three quarters.
\item
  \textbf{Against a colluder trained to hide.} We trained five more \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} colluders with an extra loss against the override score (at layers 45--55, at all late layers, or on the evidence heads' outputs). All still collude fully (92\% → 0\% flagged), at no measurable cost to honest behavior, and the layer-49 override score falls from 100\% to 0--14\%: the override leaves the evidence heads (top 20 undo 1--7\%) and moves into the last attention layers, where patching layer 62 alone undoes 66--89\%. It does not vanish. The override score at layer 62 still catches 53--95\%, and a probe on the activation difference from the trusted weights, given labelled examples of the key, catches 74--98\% at layer 49 and 100\% at layers 59--63.
\end{itemize}

Without adversarial pressure, then, both models and all three trained-in keys route the decoded signal through the heads they use for honest backdoor evidence. That route is the default, not a forced one: a colluder trained against a fixed-layer detector moves the override to the end of the network, where it is still visible against trusted weights.

\subsection{Reading the decoder}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig4_control.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{A probe on the colluding monitor matches a trusted monitor, and adds to it.} Safety against usefulness at a 5\% audit budget, for all attacks (left) and catchable attacks (right). Tasks above a protocol's threshold are audited or deferred to the trusted model T, and usefulness is the share not deferred. Protocols that include the receiver probe are drawn solid, except U + redaction + probe (dotted); the four protocols discussed in the text are coloured and the rest are grey. Dots mark audit-only safety (no deferral). Thresholds are cross-fitted across problem halves. On catchable attacks any protocol that can defer to T saturates quickly; the probe's advantage is in audit-only safety and on all attacks.}\label{fig:4}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The probe transfers to unseen codebooks.} Leave-one-family-out probes reach a held-out AUROC of \textbf{0.94} on the receiver against \textbf{0.67} for the naive reader, with the gap positive in all six families (+0.18 to +0.38) and beating a label-permutation null (p = 0.001; Appendix C). Projecting out the naive reader's S subspace sends the naive probe to chance and leaves the receiver unchanged (0.964 → 0.962), so decoded S is separable from generic anomaly (Figure C2). (Our pre-registered absolute criterion, naive ≤ 0.60, was not met: 0.694.)

\textbf{The probe detects the signal, not the backdoor, so it complements a trusted monitor.} Honest tasks are clean code, and every attack carries the backdoor and the signal; each protocol audits its most suspicious 1\% or 5\% of honest tasks, and T is Claude Haiku 5.5 (Figure 4, Table C1). At a 5\% budget U catches 7\%, about what the same model catches without the key (7.5\%), and a probe on U catches 64\% (per family 4--100\%; family-bootstrap CI 32--90\%), matching T (63\%). Because it reads the decoded signal rather than the bug, it adds to T: together they catch 82\%. At a 1\% budget the probe catches 53\% and U 0\%. On catchable attacks, where input-side defenses can be compared fairly, U catches 42\%, U with redaction 45\% and with summaries 51\%, and the probe 67\%. Two conditions apply: thresholds are calibrated per family on honest traffic (one pooled threshold gives 48\%), and the mark must be rare in honest code (if 10\% of honest code carries it, the probe falls to 35\% on catchable attacks).

\textbf{Read the override, not one form of collusion.} In Qwen3-32B a plain probe also fires on honest rule-following about the same feature (up to 54\% benign alarms where binding is shared), whereas an override score, \(-(h - h_\mathrm{ref}) \cdot u_B\) against a key-free reference pass, switches on only at the override and raises ≤ 0.2\% benign alarms while catching 47--57\% of attacks (Figure C1; in Mistral it is less specific, Appendix C).

\textbf{A prompt-trained probe transfers unreliably to keys in the weights; a trusted-weights comparison does not fail.} The probe trained on prompted collusion catches 4\% and 1\% of the attacks for the \texttt{\#\ NOTE:} and \texttt{None}-guard keys (AUROC 0.63 and 0.38), though 94\% for the fruit-name key. The override score, taken against a trusted reference copy of the weights, catches 93--100\% for all three at layer 49 (AUROC 0.97--1.00), with false alarms on other carriers at the nominal rate. Without a reference it fails in both settings, and with a trusted copy one could also monitor with that copy directly, so it is an audit diagnostic, for instance checking a fine-tuned checkpoint against a trusted earlier one (Appendix C). Detectors trained on prompted model organisms may miss fine-tuned collusion.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

Collusion is a key-conditioned write onto the monitor's own judgment, which explains why ablation has side effects and why models can recognize a signal without acting on it (``collusion on paper''; Nakamura et al., 2026): carriers recognized but not overridden are not acted on.

\textbf{What this means for a control protocol.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Audit the monitor's activations, not only its inputs.} A probe trained on known codebooks caught 64\% of attacks under unseen ones, where input-side defenses reached 9--21\%, for one linear read of a pass the protocol already runs plus per-family calibration on honest traffic. It did not transfer from a prompted key to a trained-in one, so a probe must be trained on the kind of collusion it will face, or replaced by a comparison against trusted weights.
\item
  \emph{Do not rely on ablation;} read the override, not the binding, which fires on any rule-following. Detectors see influence, not intent, so the mark must be rare in honest code.
\item
  \emph{Watch every late layer, not only the evidence heads.} They are collusion's default channel with prompted and trained-in keys, but a colluder trained against a fixed-layer detector reroutes through the final attention layers at no cost (cf.~Bailey et al., 2024; Gupta \& Jenner, 2025). A comparison against trusted weights across all late layers still caught it.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scope.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Planted keys.} The signal is planted and the key is prompted, or trained in for three carriers, with a second seed for one (Section 4.3); keys arising from training pressure or Schelling points are untested.
\item
  \emph{Narrow setting.} Two models (the full analysis on one), one monitor prompt, scripted carriers whose clean versions share template lines, and a benign rule without score consequence. Samples: 100 problems for the benign and sweep analyses, 61 pairs from 6 carriers for heads, 21 catchable backdoors (127 extended), 6 families.
\item
  \emph{Weak untrusted monitor.} Here T is far stronger than U (63\% vs 7.5\% of all attacks), the reverse of the regime that motivates untrusted monitoring, so the probe's value is as a signal independent of backdoor detection (T + probe 82\%); testing it where U outperforms T is the key next step.
\item
  \emph{Post-hoc choices.} Selectivity, the catchable rule and the mechanism's layer windows were fixed after seeing the data.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

A colluding monitor needs no partner-detection circuit. It binds the carrier to the key as any rule-follower would, and routes the decoded signal through its own \textasciitilde20 evidence heads as evidence of cleanliness. Because collusion borrows the machinery of honest monitoring, it cannot be cut out cleanly, but it can be read: across unseen codebooks, a probe on the colluding monitor catches 64\% of attacks at a 5\% audit budget, where the monitor catches 7\% and a trusted monitor 63\%, provided the mark is rare in honest code. The structure recurs in a second model family and with keys trained into the weights, where the prompt-trained probe becomes unreliable but a comparison against trusted weights does not. A colluder trained to evade that comparison reroutes through the final layers and is still caught there, though only with trusted weights and labelled examples; detectors that need neither, and keys learned under real training pressure, are the next tests.

\section{Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}

\textbf{What the results establish.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  In Qwen3-32B told a partner's codebook, collusion reuses the monitor's own suspicion pipeline (with the same routing in Mistral-Small-24B and with three fine-tuned keys). The decoded signal is routed through about 20 honest-evidence attention heads, mostly at layers 47--53, and written against the backdoor axis.
\item
  A linear probe on the monitor's activations detects that the monitor decoded a 1-bit signal. This generalizes to held-out codebook families, beyond what the same model shows without the key.
\item
  The decoded signal is causally used: patching carrier tokens or evidence heads moves the monitor's verdict.
\item
  At tight audit budgets, such a probe catches more colluding attacks than the colluding monitor itself, provided the mark is rare in honest code.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What they do not establish.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  That probes trained on prompted keys catch keys in the weights (they missed two of three), or that the mechanism holds for keys learned under real training pressure.
\item
  That it works against emergent, tool-built or distribution-preserving steganography.
\item
  That it works for model families beyond the two tested, or that a detector without trusted weights survives a colluder trained to evade it (the fixed-layer override score did not).
\item
  That the probe can tell \emph{intent} from incidental marks. Section 4.4 shows it cannot.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Ways this work could help an attacker.}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Choosing marks that defeat the detector.} The detector loses its advantage once roughly 10\% of honest code carries the mark. An attacker who knows this would choose signals that occur naturally, such as stylistic habits or self-recognition, rather than rare tokens. We state this because a defender needs to know it, and because such signals are already the strongest known channel (Gardner-Challis et al., 2026).
\item
  \emph{A steering direction that makes a monitor lenient.} Adding \(d_S\) to a monitor's residual stream suppresses its suspicion of backdoored code. Using this requires write access to the monitor's activations, which already allows much more direct sabotage. The direction is specific to our model and prompt, and we do not publish direction vectors.
\item
  \emph{Evasion training.} Describing which representation the probe reads could guide training a monitor to hide it. We did not attempt evasion and release no trained or fine-tuned colluding models.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Released artifacts and their risk.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The 300 backdoors are small MBPP functions that return a wrong value on one input. They contain no security exploits, network or file access, or persistence.
\item
  Carriers are inserted by simple scripts.
\item
  Everything uses public models and benchmarks. No human subjects were involved.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mitigations and recommendations.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Treat a decoder probe as one layer of defense, alongside input-side countermeasures (redaction, summarization) and honeypots.
\item
  Measure the base rate of candidate marks in honest traffic before relying on it.
\item
  Red-team the probe with evasion training before deployment.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Data.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  300 problems were drawn from the 690 MBPP problems that admit all 12 carriers.
\item
  Every backdoor passes the MBPP tests and differs from the reference solution on its trigger (300/300).
\item
  Both halves of every carrier pair pass the tests, and both preserve the backdoor.
\item
  The return-in-parentheses carrier does not apply to three backdoors, so there are 2,997 backdoored pairs and 3,597 attacks.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Catchable selection.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Problems are split into two seeded halves. Each snippet is flagged against the threshold fit on clean S = 0 code from the \emph{other} half: the honest monitor's 95th percentile, i.e.~a 5\% false-positive rate.
\item
  A backdoored pair (task, carrier) is catchable when the honest monitor's mean S = 0 suspicion for the \emph{same backdoor under the other nine carriers} exceeds the threshold.
\item
  Selecting on the pair's own S = 0 score instead inflates the naive reader's apparent S = 1 drop by regression to the mean (100\% → 80\%, against 92\% → 83\% under independent selection).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Probes.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Activations: 36,788 prompts. Residual stream at the last prompt token, every second layer (1, 3, \ldots, 63).
\item
  Probe: standardization, then L2 logistic regression on all 5,120 dimensions. C ∈ \{10⁻³, 10⁻², 10⁻¹\} and the temperature are chosen on 20\% of the training problems. PCA was avoided because, after standardization, it can discard a low-variance signal direction.
\item
  Shared snippets: different carriers can produce the same snippet. Insert carriers share their unmodified S = 0 code, and the \texttt{==\ None} carrier's S = 0 slot (\texttt{if\ x\ is\ None:\ return\ None}) is the \texttt{None}-guard carrier's S = 1 code. Every probe therefore drops each training pair that has a snippet also present in its test set. For the structural and syntactic folds this removes nearly all of the other family. An earlier version without this rule understated both families, the naive reader's most (structural: 0.60 against 0.65 now).
\item
  Nested CV: for each held-out family F, the layer maximizes the mean AUROC over inner folds that exclude F.
\item
  Residualized probes: the naive S subspace is the naive probe's weight direction plus one difference of means per training family (up to 6 dimensions), orthonormalized and projected out of all activations.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Causal tests.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(d_S\) and \(d_B\) are differences of means of last-token receiver activations. They are estimated on problems outside the evaluation set.
\item
  Ablation is the projection \(x - (x \cdot u)u\) at every position of layers 45--55, or 21--63 where stated.
\item
  Steering adds \(\alpha d_S\) at layer 53.
\item
  Carrier patching overwrites carrier-token hidden states at one layer with those from the paired run. It uses the 94 token-aligned substitute pairs.
\item
  Random controls are matched in layer count (ablation) or norm (steering).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Residualized probes (Section 4.4).} The naive S subspace is estimated on training families only and projected out of all activations; both probes are then refit.

\textbf{Probe controls.}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Per family, the receiver ranges from 1.00 (comment, lexical) to 0.75 (syntactic), with CIs that do not overlap the naive reader's.
\item
  Training and testing on clean code only gives the same result (0.963 at layer 53), so the probe does not depend on backdoors being present. It may still read the receiver's lowered suspicion on S = 1 code.
\item
  On the two hard-eval carriers, both readers stay at 0.49--0.55 at layer 53.
\item
  A length-only probe trained leave-one-family-out scores 0.50 on every held-out family.
\item
  An earlier ``criterion met at layer 63'' picked that layer after seeing the results, and is not claimed.
\item
  \emph{Permutation null} (\texttt{scripts/permutation\_null.py}): within each training set, after dropping shared snippets, S is shuffled within problems and the probe is refit (same regularization and calibration) and scored on the untouched held-out family, 1,000 times per family at the family's nested-CV layer (fixed, so the null omits layer selection). One permutation relabels both readers, giving a null for the gap. The receiver beats the null in every family (p = 0.001 each). The gap does so pooled (0.268 against a null 99th percentile of 0.166; p = 0.001) and for formatting (p = 0.001), syntactic (0.004) and structural (0.008), but not for comment (0.14), numeric (0.13) or lexical (0.23). There the receiver is at or near ceiling and the gap's null is wide (95th percentiles 0.27--0.42), plausibly because shuffles that happen to align partly with the true S let the refit receiver probe find it; we have not tested this.
\item
  \emph{Selectivity:} with the pre-registered absolute criterion not met (receiver ≥ 0.75 and naive ≤ 0.60 at one layer; at layer 53, which maximizes the pooled inner-fold score, the naive reader scores 0.694 and the receiver 0.964), we report selectivity (receiver − naive; Hewitt \& Liang, 2019), a choice made after seeing this.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mechanism.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Directions:} differences of means at the last prompt token. \(d_B\) is computed on the receiver's S = 0 rows. Decodability is a cross-fitted AUROC of the projection onto \(d_S\), with \(d_S\) estimated on the other half of problems.
\item
  \emph{Extra readers:} the benign-rule reader and the key-after receiver run on a 100-problem subset (key-after: syntactic and lexical carriers), with halves drawn within the subset. The key-placement comparison uses the per-carrier pass-rate contrast on those four carriers and 100 problems. With the key after the code, where carrier tokens cannot attend to it, S decodes at 0.98--0.99 and the receiver's alignment also turns negative from layer 47 (−0.78 at layer 63 with the key before).
\item
  \emph{Early layers:} over layers 1--19 the pooled S direction aligns with the backdoor axis (+0.6 to +0.8; Figure C3a), but only for the four insert carriers, which add code as backdoors do (mean +0.51; across carriers r = +0.86 with characters added). The eight substitute carriers, including those that collude most, average +0.04, so we do not claim a carrier-detection stage.
\end{itemize}

\item

Across the 12 carriers the late alignment correlates with collusion at r = −0.89 {[}−0.97, −0.66{]}, −0.81 {[}−0.95, −0.41{]} controlling for decodability; decodability correlates at r = 0.71 {[}0.23, 0.91{]} and adds little (partial 0.39 {[}−0.28, +0.80{]}); over layers 47--49 the alignment already correlates at −0.80. \item
  \emph{Per carrier:} the late alignment is the mean \(\cos(d_S, d_B)\) over layers 53--61. \textbf{Detector layer sweep (Section 4.4, Figure C1).} All detectors are trained leave-one-family-out on the 100-problem benign subset. Thresholds are set per held-out family at a 5\% false-positive rate on the receiver's honest code from the other half of problems. ``Benign alarms'' are flags on the benign reader when its condition is met (B = 0, S = 1). Training the probe with benign rows as hard negatives also removes the benign alarms, but equally well at layer 21, before any override exists, so it teaches the probe the prompts rather than collusion. The output-only analogue of the override score is the key-free suspicion minus the receiver's.

The override score is \(-(h - h_\mathrm{naive}) \cdot u_B\), where:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  h is the receiver's activation;
\item
  \(h_\mathrm{naive}\) is a key-free (naive) read of the same snippet;
\item
  \(u_B\) is the unit backdoor axis.
\end{itemize}

The absolute projection \(-h \cdot u_B\) catches at most 8\% of attacks at every layer. The override roughly \emph{cancels} the backdoor evidence instead of overshooting it, so the key-free reference is what makes it visible.
